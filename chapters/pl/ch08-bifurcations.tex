\chapter{Droga do chaosu i liczba, której nikt się nie spodziewał}
\label{ch:bifurcations}

\chapterstub{%
\textit{[Opis do przetłumaczenia --- patrz wydanie angielskie.]}

Argument: the whole of Chapter 5 fits in one picture \dash{} the bifurcation
diagram, the long-run values of x plotted against r \dash{} and the picture
has structure. Period one splits to two, two to four, four to eight, at
parameter values that crowd together geometrically; the ratio of successive
gaps tends to Feigenbaum's constant, about 4.669, which the reader computes
from the map's superstable parameters. The same number comes out of a
completely different rule (the sine map \code{x[n+1] = s*sin(pi*x[n])}),
which is universality: the number belongs to the route, not to the equation
\dash{} and it has been measured in real fluids and circuits, within the
precision of those experiments. Inside the chaos there are windows of order;
the period-3 window opens at \code{r = 1 + sqrt(8)}, and Li and Yorke's 1975
paper title, period three implies chaos, says what that means. Payoff: the
reader can draw a bifurcation diagram, compute the first few period-doubling
parameters and Feigenbaum's ratio, and explain why a constant of nature can
come out of a toy model. Traps to elicit: the transition from order to chaos
is a sudden switch; a different equation gives a different route; chaos,
once reached, is everywhere beyond it. Listings: the bifurcation data;
superstable parameters by bisection; the ratios for two maps. Labs: locate a
superstable parameter; compute a Feigenbaum ratio. Figures: the bifurcation
diagram; a zoom showing self-similarity; the ratios converging.
