\chapter{Chaos wewnątrz komputera}
\label{ch:computers}

\chapterstub{%
\textit{[Opis do przetłumaczenia --- patrz wydanie angielskie.]}

Argument: a computer simulating a chaotic system is itself a slightly
different system, because every step rounds. Floating-point numbers are
introduced from zero (a fixed number of significant binary digits), and the
logistic map run in single and in double precision parts company after a
predictable number of steps \dash{} the prediction horizon of Chapter 7 with
the rounding error as the initial error. The doubling map \code{x -> 2x mod
1} run on doubles collapses to exactly zero after about fifty-odd steps,
because each step shifts out one binary digit and the machine has only
fifty-three \dash{} a chaotic map that the computer cannot even represent.
And yet simulations are not worthless: the shadowing idea says that for many
chaotic systems a computed orbit stays close to SOME true orbit from a
slightly different start, so statistics come out right even when the path is
wrong \dash{} which is why Chapter 9's lobe fractions survive.
Reproducibility: the same code on two machines can diverge when a single
operation rounds differently, which is why this book's own numbers use only
correctly rounded arithmetic and are checked in CI. Where this meets AI:
training a network multiplies many Jacobians, and the growth rate of that
product is a Lyapunov exponent \dash{} exploding and vanishing gradients are
chaos and its opposite; random recurrent networks have a transition to chaos
(Sompolinsky, Crisanti and Sommers, 1988); pseudo-random numbers are
deliberately chaotic arithmetic. Payoff: the reader can predict when a
float32 simulation will part from a float64 one, explain the doubling-map
collapse, and say which results of a chaotic simulation can be trusted.
Traps to elicit: a deterministic program always gives the same answer; more
decimal places make a simulation exact; a chaotic map makes a good random-
number generator. Listings: float32 against float64 logistic; the doubling
map collapse; shadowing illustrated by statistics that agree; a product of
random matrices growing. Labs: count steps until float32 and float64
disagree; predict the doubling-map collapse step. Figures: two precisions
parting company; the product of Jacobians growing or shrinking.
