\chapter{Iteracja na płaszczyźnie: Julia i Mandelbrot}
\label{ch:mandelbrot}

\chapterstub{%
\textit{[Opis do przetłumaczenia --- patrz wydanie angielskie.]}

Argument: iterate the simplest nonlinear rule, \code{z[n+1] = z[n]*z[n] +
c}, on points of a plane instead of a line, and the question will this
escape to infinity draws the most famous shapes in mathematics. Complex
numbers are introduced from zero as points of the plane with a rule for
multiplying them (lengths multiply, angles add) and Python's built-in
complex type does the arithmetic. For a fixed c, the points that do not
escape form a Julia set (Julia and Fatou, around 1918); the set of c for
which the orbit of zero does not escape is the Mandelbrot set, drawn by
computer from about 1980. The escape radius 2 is proved in one line. The
payoff connects the book: the logistic map is the same rule in disguise (a
change of variables turns \code{r*x*(1-x)} into \code{z*z + c} with \code{c
= r/2 - r*r/4}), so the real axis of the Mandelbrot set IS Chapter 8's
bifurcation diagram \dash{} the period-doubling points and the period-3
window sit exactly where the conversion says, checked numerically. Payoff:
the reader can compute escape times, draw a Julia set and the Mandelbrot set
at low resolution, and convert between r and c. Traps to elicit: complex
numbers are imaginary so they describe nothing real; the Mandelbrot set is a
picture of chaos (its boundary is where chaos lives); infinite detail means
infinite information. Listings: complex multiplication by hand and by
Python; escape time; the r-to-c conversion checked against Chapter 8's
numbers. Labs: implement escape time; convert Feigenbaum points to c.
Figures: the Mandelbrot set; two Julia sets, one connected and one dust; the
real axis against the bifurcation diagram.
