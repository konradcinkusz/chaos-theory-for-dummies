\chapter{Fraktale: mierzenie czegoś, co nie ma długości}
\label{ch:fractals}

\chapterstub{%
\textit{[Opis do przetłumaczenia --- patrz wydanie angielskie.]}

Argument: the shapes chaos leaves behind are fractals, and fractals break
the idea of length. The Cantor set (1883) and Koch's snowflake curve (1904)
are built by iteration; the Koch curve's length multiplies by 4/3 at every
stage, so it has no finite length while enclosing a finite area. Lewis Fry
Richardson's measurements of coastlines and borders and Mandelbrot's 1967
paper in Science asking how long the coast of Britain is: the measured
length grows as the ruler shrinks, along a straight line on a log-log plot
whose slope gives a dimension between one and two. Dimension becomes a
measurement \dash{} box counting \dash{} and the reader computes it for the
Koch curve (log 4 / log 3, about 1.26) and for Chapter 10's Henon attractor
(also about 1.26, by coincidence, which the chapter says). Nature's
fractals, stated carefully: coastlines, river networks, lungs and blood
vessels are fractal over a range of scales, not all the way down. Payoff:
the reader can compute a box-counting dimension, read a log-log plot, and
say what a non-integer dimension claims. Traps to elicit: a coastline has a
length; dimension must be a whole number; fractals are decoration. Listings:
building the Koch curve and counting its length; box counting on the Koch
curve and on the Henon attractor. Labs: implement box counting; compute the
Cantor set's dimension. Figures: Koch stages; the log-log plot of boxes
against box size.
