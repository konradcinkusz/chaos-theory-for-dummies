\chapter{Okiełznać chaos: sterowanie i synchronizacja}
\label{ch:taming}

\chapterstub{%
\textit{[Opis do przetłumaczenia --- patrz wydanie angielskie.]}

Argument: sensitivity cuts both ways. If a tiny disturbance changes the
future a lot, then a tiny, well-timed push can steer it a lot \dash{} which
is the idea of Ott, Grebogi and Yorke's 1990 paper Controlling Chaos. A
chaotic attractor is full of unstable periodic orbits; wait until the state
passes near one and nudge the parameter slightly, and the system can be held
on an orbit it would never keep by itself. The reader stabilises the
logistic map's unstable fixed point at r = 3.9 with parameter nudges of
under one per cent, and measures how small the nudges are. Synchronisation:
two identical chaotic systems, one driving the other through a single
variable, lock together (Pecora and Carroll, 1990), shown on the Lorenz
system \dash{} the idea behind proposals to hide a message in chaotic noise.
The chapter keeps claims in proportion: control of chaos has been
demonstrated in lasers, circuits and heart tissue in the laboratory; it is
not a routine clinical or industrial technique, and the chapter says so.
Payoff: the reader can implement a small-nudge controller, show it
stabilising an unstable orbit, and synchronise two Lorenz systems through
one coupling. Traps to elicit: chaos cannot be controlled; controlling a
system means overpowering it; two chaotic systems can never agree. Listings:
the uncontrolled and controlled logistic map; nudge sizes; drive-response
Lorenz synchronisation error. Labs: implement the controller rule; measure
synchronisation time. Figures: control switched on mid-run; synchronisation
error falling to zero.
