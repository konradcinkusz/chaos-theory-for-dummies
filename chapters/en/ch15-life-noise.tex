\chapter{Life, markets and noise: telling chaos from chance}
\label{ch:life}
\index{chaos!telling from noise}\index{noise}

\noindent
A cardiologist looks at the gaps between heartbeats, an epidemiologist at a
century of monthly measles counts, a trader at a price. Each sees a wiggly
line that never quite repeats, and wants to know what makes it wiggle: a
rule, with a few numbers pushing each other about, or chance, countless small
causes no model will ever list. Most often it is some of both.

Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic} showed that a rule one line long can produce output
as irregular as a lottery. This chapter asks the question the other way
round: handed a wiggly series and no rule, can you tell whether a rule made
it? You will build two tests, measure how well they work when the data are
noisy and short, and then visit the places where chaos has been claimed. The
message is not that chaos is everywhere, nor that it is nowhere. It is that a
claim of chaos can be checked.

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{make a chaotic series and a random one out of exactly the same
  numbers, and say why no histogram can tell them apart}
\outcome{draw a return map, count the squares it lands in, and read the
  count}
\outcome{forecast a series from its own past by the method of analogues,
  and measure how the error grows with the number of steps ahead}
\outcome{say how measurement noise and a short record weaken both tests}
\outcome{judge a claim of chaos in populations, epidemics, hearts or markets
  by the evidence offered for it}
\end{outcomes}

\section{Two series, one set of numbers}
\label{sec:ch15-twins}
\index{surrogate data}

\noindent
Start with a puzzle. Two series each hold \val{ch15.n} numbers between $0$
and $1$. One was made by the logistic rule of Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic} with
its knob at $r = 4$: if $x_n$ is the value at step $n$, the next one is
\[ x_{n+1} = 4\,x_n\,(1 - x_n), \]
four times the value times one minus the value. The other series is random.
Both are printed below to four decimal places; the program that made them
keeps about sixteen significant figures.

\begin{think}
Series~A begins \val{ch15.a.1}, \val{ch15.a.2}, \val{ch15.a.3},
\val{ch15.a.4}. Series~B begins \val{ch15.b.1}, \val{ch15.b.2},
\val{ch15.b.3}, \val{ch15.b.4}. Which one did the rule make? Can you tell by
looking, and can you tell by a calculation?
\end{think}
\reveal{B. By eye you cannot tell. By calculation you can: $4 \times
\num{0.2} \times \num{0.8} = \num{0.64}$, which is B's second value, and
$4 \times \num{0.64} \times \num{0.36}$ is \val{ch15.b.3}, its third. For A
the rule gives about \val{ch15.a.next} from the first value, and the second
value is \val{ch15.a.2}.}
Look at what the test used. Not the size of the numbers, not how often each
size turns up, but their \emph{order}: whether each value follows from the
one before. That is the whole of this chapter.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A random-looking sequence must come from randomness.}}
Series~B looked every bit as random as A, and it came from one line of
arithmetic with no chance in it. Looking random is a fact about the look. It
says nothing about the cause until you have tested for the kind of order a
rule leaves behind.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
Series~A was not made by rolling dice. It was made from series~B, by
shuffling:

\pyregion{code/ch15/twins.py}{make}{Two series from one: a rule, and the same numbers shuffled}{lst:ch15-make}

\noindent
The first function iterates the rule; the second shuffles a copy of the
list with Python's random number generator, started from a fixed
\emph{seed} so that the shuffle is the same on every computer and your run
matches the page. A series built from another by scrambling its order is
called a \emph{surrogate}: the hardest random
series to tell from the original, because it has exactly the same numbers.

\begin{think}
How will the histogram of the shuffled series \dash{} how many of its values
fall in each fifth of the range from $0$ to $1$ \dash{} compare with that of
the rule's series?
\end{think}
\reveal{Identical, bar for bar. A histogram counts values and ignores their
order, and a shuffle changes only the order.}

\transcript{ch15-twins}

\noindent
Note the shape: \val{ch15.hist.low} of the \val{ch15.n} values sit in the
lowest fifth and only \val{ch15.hist.mid} in the middle one, because the
rule lingers near $0$ and $1$. The shuffle has the same lopsided histogram,
and the same average, spread, largest and smallest value. No summary that
ignores order can tell the twins apart.

\plotfig{ch15-twins}{The rule's series and its shuffle. Top: the first
hundred steps of each, equally ragged. Bottom: their histograms, which are
identical.}{two series with one histogram}

\section{Plot each value against the next}
\label{sec:ch15-return}
\index{return map}

\noindent
In the puzzle you could test each value against the one before because you
were told the rule. With real data nobody tells you, so let the data draw the
rule for you. Put a point at $(x_n, x_{n+1})$ for every step $n$: across by
this value, up by the next. The picture is called a \emph{return map}.

\begin{think}
Where will the points of the rule's return map lie? And the shuffle's?
\end{think}
\reveal{The rule's points lie on one curve, the hump of $y = 4x(1 - x)$,
because every next value \emph{is} the rule applied to the value before. The
shuffle's points spread over the whole square, because in a shuffle the next
value has nothing to do with this one.}

\plotfig{ch15-return-maps}{Each value plotted against the next. The rule's
series draws its own rule; the shuffled series fills the square. The faint
lines cut the square into a hundred little squares.}{a curve and a cloud}

\noindent
A picture is a judgement; a count is a measurement. Cut the square into a
hundred little squares and count how many the return map lands in:

\pyregion{code/ch15/return_map.py}{pairs}{Pairs of neighbours, and the squares they land in}{lst:ch15-pairs}

\transcript{ch15-return-map}

\noindent
A curve can pass through only so many squares. The rule's \val{ch15.n}
values touch \val{ch15.sq.rule}, and a longer record would not raise the
count, because every new point lands on the same curve. The shuffle keeps
finding new squares until it has touched all \val{ch15.sq.shuf}.

\begin{lab}{l15_01_return}{Curve or cloud?}
Write the return map test yourself: pair each value with the next, count the
squares the pairs land in, and return \code{"curve"} if fewer than half the
squares are touched and \code{"cloud"} otherwise. Then try a grid of two by
two on a series that cycles through three values, and see how a grid that is
too coarse misleads.
\end{lab}

\begin{worldbox}
Robert Shaw, a physicist at the University of California at Santa Cruz,
timed the drops falling from a leaking tap. At some rates of flow the drops
came at irregular intervals, and when he plotted each interval against the
next, the points did not fill a cloud: they lay along a thin curved shape. A
kitchen tap became one of the best-known demonstrations of chaos read from a
single measured quantity.
\end{worldbox}

\noindent
Shaw measured one thing, the gap between drops, though the water has far more
going on than one number describes. That he saw a shape is not luck. In 1980
Norman Packard, James Crutchfield, Doyne Farmer and Shaw himself showed how
to rebuild the shape of a system's motion from one measured quantity, by
using delayed copies of it \dash{} the reading now, a moment ago, a moment
before that \dash{} as stand-ins for the numbers nobody measured. In 1981
Floris Takens proved that this works. The return map is the simplest case:
one quantity, one delay.

\begin{rigourbox}
Takens' theorem in words: for a smooth deterministic system whose state has
$d$ numbers, $2d + 1$ delayed copies of almost any single measurement give a
faithful copy of the motion, bent and stretched but with the same shape. The
proof, in his 1981 paper \emph{Detecting strange attractors in turbulence},
uses differential topology.
\end{rigourbox}

\section{Forecasting from the past}
\label{sec:ch15-analogues}
\index{method of analogues}\index{nearest neighbour}

\noindent
The second test asks what a user of the data cares about: can I predict it?
With no rule, you can search the record for the moment most like now and
assume that what happened next then will happen next now. This is the
\emph{method of analogues}. In 1969 Edward Lorenz tried it on weather maps
and found no past day close enough to be useful: with so many numbers in its
state, the atmosphere had not come back near where it had been. For a series
of single numbers it works:

\pyregion{code/ch15/analogues.py}{forecast}{Forecasting by the nearest past analogue}{lst:ch15-forecast}

\noindent
\code{forecast} finds the past value closest to \code{x}, its \emph{nearest
neighbour}, and returns what came \code{h} steps after it.
\code{mean\_error} learns from the first thousand values, forecasts every
value after them, and averages the sizes of the misses.

\begin{think}
For the rule's series the average miss one step ahead is about
\val{ch15.fc.rule}, a thousandth. A blind guess \dash{} any value picked from
the histogram \dash{} misses by about \val{ch15.fc.shuf}.
Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov} measured that at $r = 4$ a small error roughly
doubles at every step. About how many steps ahead can the forecast go before
its misses reach half of a blind guess's?
\end{think}
\reveal{About nine: a thousandth doubled nine times is about a half, since
$2^{9} = 512$. The measurement agrees: the misses first pass half of the
shuffle's at step \val{ch15.fc.half}.}

\transcript{ch15-analogues}

\plotfig{ch15-forecast}{The average miss of a forecast by analogues against
the number of steps ahead, on a logarithmic scale. The shuffle is a blind
guess at every horizon; the rule's series is forecast well, and less well at
every step.}{forecast error against steps ahead}

\noindent
For the shuffled series the forecast misses by about \val{ch15.fc.shuf} at
every horizon: its past holds no information about its next value. For the
rule's series the miss one step ahead is about \val{ch15.fc.times} times
smaller, and it grows by a factor of about \val{ch15.fc.double} a step
\dash{} the stretching Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov} measured from the inside,
seen from the outside \dash{} until after a dozen steps it too is a blind
guess.

So chaos and noise differ in both directions. Noise is equally unpredictable
at every horizon. Chaos is predictable a few steps ahead, where noise is not,
and loses that at a steady, measurable rate: a stronger fingerprint of chaos
than irregularity will ever be.

\section{Real data is a mix}
\label{sec:ch15-noisy}
\index{noise!measurement}

\noindent
Nothing is measured exactly. A scale, a thermometer or a clerk copying a
register moves each value a little, at random. To see what that does, move
each value of the rule's series by a random amount no bigger than some size:

\pyregion{code/ch15/noisy.py}{noise}{Measurement noise: each value moved at random}{lst:ch15-noise}

\begin{think}
Add noise of up to \val{ch15.noise.small} to the rule's series: a hundredth,
thinner than the curve in the return map as printed. What happens to the
average miss one step ahead, which was \val{ch15.fc.rule}?
\end{think}
\reveal{It rises to \val{ch15.noise.small.err}, many times worse. The
analogue the forecast finds is itself off by up to a hundredth, and so is the
value being forecast, before the rule's doubling has even started.}

\plotfig{ch15-noisy}{Return maps of the rule's series seen through
measurement noise of three sizes. The curve thickens into a band, and at the
largest size the band nearly fills the square.}{a curve thickening in noise}

\transcript{ch15-noisy}

\noindent
In the upper table, the whole record, the curve thickens into a band as the
noise grows: with noise of up to \val{ch15.noise.big}, a fifth of the whole
range, it touches \val{ch15.noise.big.sq} squares. Even so, with
\val{ch15.n} values the forecast still beats a blind guess,
\val{ch15.noise.big.err} against \val{ch15.fc.shuf}.

The lower table is the cruel part. It cuts the record into short pieces and
counts, out of a hundred, how often each test says the rule's piece is less
random than its own shuffle. With forty noisy values the forecast test is
right \val{ch15.short.fc.forty} times. With ten it is right
\val{ch15.short.fc} times, and the squares test only \val{ch15.short.sq}
\dash{} worse than tossing a coin. Short, noisy data can make order look like
chance, and chance look like order.
And this is the easiest chaos there is, with one number per step; a system
with many numbers in its state needs a far longer record.

\begin{lab}{l15_02_forecast}{Ask several analogues}
With noise in the record, the closest analogue may be close by accident.
Write a forecaster that finds the few closest past values instead and
averages what came after them. The test checks it on a small library you can
work by hand, and then that averaging five analogues beats trusting one on a
noisy series.
\end{lab}

\section{Populations, epidemics, hearts}
\label{sec:ch15-life}
\index{population dynamics}\index{epidemic}\index{SIR model}

\noindent
Robert May's 1976 paper on the logistic map was written for ecologists. Its
lesson was that a population jumping about irregularly from year to year
need not be pushed by weather or luck: its own breeding rule could do it.

\begin{think}
Many real insect populations do rise and fall irregularly from one year to
the next. Does that make them chaotic?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. Irregular numbers are what chaos produces, and also what a steady
population buffeted by weather, disease and chance produces. Irregularity
cannot tell the two apart; only tests of order can.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Irregular data proves chaos.}} Every series in this chapter
was irregular, and half of them were shuffles. Chaos is the claim that a rule
with few numbers in it makes the irregularity, and it needs evidence of that
rule: structure in the return map, a forecast that starts good and worsens
steadily, and a record long and clean enough for either to mean something.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
The strongest evidence has come from the laboratory, where the rule can be
changed on purpose. In 1997 Robert Costantino and colleagues reported in
\emph{Science} on flour beetles in jars, whose rates of adult death and
recruitment the experimenters set by hand. As those rates changed, the
colonies moved between steady numbers, cycles and irregular swings where
their model predicted. A wild population counted yearly for a few decades
gives about as few values as the lower table above.

Epidemics are the other classic. The \emph{SIR model} divides people into
the susceptible, who can catch the disease, the infectious, who have it, and
the recovered. New infections each day are proportional to how many
susceptible and infectious people there are to meet, a case stays infectious
for \val{ch15.sir.days} days on average, and births bring in new
susceptibles. Take a measles-like disease, where one case among people with
no immunity infects about \val{ch15.sir.r0} others, and a lifetime of
\val{ch15.sir.life} years. Then add a school year: children mix more in term
than in the holidays, so let the contact rate rise and fall once a year by a
fraction called the \emph{season}.

\pyregion{code/ch15/epidemic.py}{rule}{An epidemic with a school year: the SIR rule stepped a quarter-day at a time}{lst:ch15-sir}

\transcript{ch15-epidemic}

\noindent
With no school year the model settles: \val{ch15.sir.steady} cases per
thousand people, every year the same. With a season of \val{ch15.sir.two},
big and small years alternate, as measles did in England and Wales before
vaccination began in 1968. At \val{ch15.sir.four} the pattern repeats every
four years, and at \val{ch15.sir.wild} it never repeats, and a nudge of one
part in a billion at the start sends the years a different way. That is the
road into chaos of Chapter~\ref{ch:bifurcations}, walked by an epidemic.

It is also where honesty is needed. In the chaotic setting the model's
epidemics burn out so completely that at the lowest point fewer than one
person in $10^{\val{ch15.sir.trough}}$ is infectious. No city has a
millionth of a person: a real disease would die out. Whether measles was ever
chaotic is still argued over. In 1990 George Sugihara and Robert May applied a forecast by analogues to
monthly measles counts from New York City and found this chapter's
signature, a good forecast that worsened steadily with the horizon, while
chickenpox in the same city looked like a yearly cycle with noise on top.

Hearts are harder still. In laboratory experiments reported in 1981, clusters
of heart cells driven by regular electrical pulses went through period
doubling into irregular beating. A healthy heart does not beat like a
metronome either, but the once popular claim that its variability is chaos
rests on this chapter's tests applied to short noisy records, and remains
contested. Variability is not the same thing as chaos.

\section{Markets, and what a claim of chaos needs}
\label{sec:ch15-evidence}
\index{markets}

\noindent
Prices are the most watched wiggly lines in the world, and for a while many
hoped that chaos was the pattern beneath them.

\begin{think}
Suppose someone proved that a share price is made by a chaotic rule with only
a few numbers in its state. Could you get rich forecasting it?
\end{think}
\reveal{Not far. Chaos is forecastable only a few steps ahead, only as well
as the measurements allow, and not at all beyond its horizon. And every
trader who found the same rule would trade on it, changing the prices it
describes.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Chaos in markets means you can predict them.}} Chaotic
prices would promise a short horizon with a hard limit behind it, not a
crystal ball. In fact economists who searched found returns hard to forecast
and calm and turbulent periods bunched together, but the evidence for a
chaotic rule with few numbers in its state is weak and contested.
Irregular, jumpy and hard to predict do not add up to chaotic.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
Put the chapter together, and a claim of chaos in real data needs:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{a long record}, long compared with the number of quantities
  that matter;
\item \textbf{little noise}, or tests that allow for it;
\item \textbf{a fair comparison}: the series must beat its surrogates,
  including surrogates that keep its slow ups and downs, because a series
  with memory, like daily temperature, is forecastable a day ahead with no
  chaos at all;
\item \textbf{the right shape of forecast error}: small a step ahead and
  growing at a steady rate;
\item and, best of all, \textbf{a mechanism}, ideally with a knob that can
  be turned, as the beetles had.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Few claims about hearts, brains or economies clear all five. That is chaos
theory doing its job: telling you what a wiggly line does and does not
license you to conclude.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{surrogate} made by shuffling has the same numbers, so the
  same \textbf{histogram}. Only the \textbf{order} can tell a rule from
  chance.
\item A \textbf{return map} plots each value against the next: a rule draws a
  curve, noise fills the square. \textbf{Delay embedding} (Takens, 1981)
  rebuilds a system's shape from one measured quantity.
\item The \textbf{method of analogues} forecasts from the nearest past value.
  Chaos is forecast well a step ahead and worse at a steady rate; noise is a
  blind guess at every horizon.
\item \textbf{Measurement noise} and \textbf{short records} weaken both tests;
  with ten noisy values the squares test does worse than a coin.
\item Chaos has been shown convincingly in laboratory populations; for
  measles, hearts and markets the evidence is mixed, contested or weak.
  \textbf{Irregular is not chaotic}.
\item A claim of chaos needs a long clean record, a fair comparison with
  surrogates, the right shape of forecast error, and ideally a mechanism.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}

\canyou

\begin{checkpoint}
\item What does shuffling a series keep, and what does it destroy?
  \answerto{It keeps every value, so the histogram. It destroys the order,
  which is where a rule linking each value to the next would show.}
\item The return map of a series lies on one thin curve. What does that tell
  you about how the series was made?
  \answerto{That each value is fixed by the one before it: a rule of one
  number made it. Noise would spread the points over the square.}
\item One series touches $30$ of the hundred squares with $500$ values and
  still $30$ with $5000$. Another touches $80$ and then $100$. Which behaves
  like a rule's series?
  \answerto{The first: a curve passes through only so many squares, however
  many points land on it. The second keeps filling the square, as noise
  does.}
\item A forecast by analogues misses by about \num{0.001} one step ahead, and
  its misses double at every step. About how many steps ahead does the miss
  reach \num{0.25}?
  \answerto{About eight: $2^{8} = 256$, and $\num{0.001} \times 256$ is
  about \num{0.26}.}
\item Why is a record of ten noisy values poor evidence for chaos or against
  it?
  \answerto{Both tests are unreliable on so little: in this chapter's
  measurement the squares test on ten noisy values was right less often than
  a tossed coin would be.}
\item What happens to the model epidemic as the school year is made stronger,
  and what makes its chaotic setting doubtful as a picture of real measles?
  \answerto{Its years go from all alike, to alternating, to repeating every
  four years, to never repeating; and its chaotic troughs are so deep that a
  real disease would die out.}
\item Someone shows you that a share price is irregular. Is that evidence of
  chaos? What would be?
  \answerto{No: noise is irregular too. Evidence would be a forecast that
  beats surrogates, with an error that starts small and grows steadily, in a
  long, clean record.}
\end{checkpoint}
