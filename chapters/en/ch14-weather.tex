\chapter{Weather, climate and the ensemble forecast}
\label{ch:weather}
\index{weather forecast}\index{ensemble forecast}

\noindent
The forecast on your phone says there is a $70$ per cent chance of rain
tomorrow afternoon. Seventy per cent of what? Not of the sky, and not of the
afternoon: nobody expects rain on seven tenths of your town for seven tenths
of the time. The number is a count, and by the end of this chapter you will
have made one yourself, on a small model of an atmosphere, and checked
whether it can be trusted.

This is where Part~II's bad news becomes a method. The atmosphere is
chaotic, so a single forecast has a horizon, and Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov}
showed that better measurements move it only a little. Forecasters could
change neither fact. What they changed was the question they answered
\dash{} from one forecast to many \dash{} and it is the most successful
practical use chaos theory has had.

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{run an ensemble forecast on a toy atmosphere and read its spread as
  a measure of confidence}
\outcome{turn an ensemble into a probability, and say how a probability
  forecast is judged}
\outcome{measure how far ahead a forecast beats quoting the climate average}
\outcome{use the rule of Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov} to say what a better start
  buys a forecast}
\outcome{tell a question about the weather from a question about the climate}
\end{outcomes}

\section{A toy atmosphere}
\label{sec:ch14-toy}
\index{Lorenz-96 model}

\noindent
A weather model divides the air into boxes, gives each box a few numbers
\dash{} temperature, pressure, wind, moisture \dash{} and uses the physics of
fluids to say how each changes. The first forecast made this way on an
electronic computer ran on ENIAC in 1950; today's global models carry many
millions of numbers.

In 1996 Edward Lorenz, whose three equations you met in
Chapter~\ref{ch:lorenz}, proposed a model small enough to think about and
big enough to behave like weather. Space $40$ numbers, $x_1$ to $x_{40}$,
round a circle of latitude, so that each stands for the weather at one place
and the last one's neighbour is the first. Read the left-hand side as
Chapter~\ref{ch:motion} taught you, as how fast $x_i$ is changing:
\[ \frac{\dd x_i}{\dd t} = (x_{i+1} - x_{i-2})\, x_{i-1} - x_i + F . \]
In words: each number is carried along by its neighbours (the product, which
moves weather round the circle as wind does), leaks away (the $-x_i$, which
plays friction), and is pushed by a steady drive $F$, the \emph{forcing},
which plays the sun. Lorenz's standard forcing is $F = \val{ch14.forcing}$,
and in \pkg{chaoslab} the whole rule is one line:

\pyregion{code/src/chaoslab/systems.py}{lorenz96}{Lorenz's forty-number toy atmosphere}{lst:ch14-lorenz96}

\noindent
The circle closes by itself: when \code{i} is $0$, Python reads
\code{s[i - 2]} as \code{s[-2]}, the second number from the end.

\begin{think}
Put every number at $8$, the value of the forcing. What does the rule say
each of them does next?
\end{think}
\reveal{Nothing. The neighbours cancel, $(8 - 8) \times 8 = 0$, and the leak
of $8$ cancels the push of $8$, so every rate of change is zero.}
A perfectly calm atmosphere stays calm. Calm is a fixed point, and as in
Chapter~\ref{ch:feedback} the question is whether it is stable. It is not:
the listings below nudge one number by a hundredth and wait, and the calm
breaks into waves that travel round the circle and never settle.

Lorenz chose his units so that one time unit is roughly five days of real
weather, which makes the listings' step of \val{ch14.dt} about six hours.
Measured with \api{lyapunov\_flow} from \pkg{chaoslab}, the exponent of
Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov} is \val{ch14.lambda} per time unit: a small error
doubles every \val{ch14.double} units, about two days.

\section{Twenty forecasts instead of one}
\label{sec:ch14-ensemble}
\index{analysis (weather)}\index{ensemble forecast!spread}

\noindent
A forecast starts from an \emph{analysis}: the best estimate of the state of
the atmosphere now, built from measurements that are never exact. Say the
analysis is right to within \val{ch14.error} on every one of the forty
numbers. You know from Chapter~\ref{ch:butterfly} what a single forecast from
it will do: follow the real weather for a while, and then not.

A weather service cannot know tomorrow's truth while it forecasts. We can,
because we play nature ourselves. Run the model once and call that run the
\emph{truth}; make an analysis by adding an error to it; forecast from the
analysis; score the forecast against the truth. Model and truth obey the
same rule, so every error below comes from the start and none from the
model: the toy has every advantage a real forecast lacks.

Now the new idea. Instead of one forecast, make \val{ch14.members}. Each
\emph{member} starts from the analysis nudged by another error of the same
size, so the members differ from one another about as much as the analysis
could differ from the truth. Together they are an \emph{ensemble}.

\pyregion{code/ch14/ensemble.py}{setup}{The toy atmosphere, a calm start, and an error of a given size}{lst:ch14-setup}

\noindent
\code{rng.uniform(-size, size)} picks a number between \code{-size} and
\code{size}, every value equally likely, so \api{perturb} adds a different
small error to each number. Three scores do the rest. The \emph{ensemble
mean} averages each number over the members. The \emph{spread} is how far
the members typically sit from that mean: square each difference, average
the squares, take the square root. The \emph{error} is the same recipe
applied to a forecast and the truth. So spread and error are the same kind
of number, measured against each other or against the truth.

\needspace{7\baselineskip}
\begin{think}
The members start within a few hundredths of one another. As the forecast
runs on, does their spread keep growing for ever?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. It grows quickly and then levels off: the members cannot end up
further apart than two unrelated days of weather, and the attractor bounds
how far apart those can be.}

\pyregion{code/ch14/ensemble.py}{forecast}{An ensemble forecast, scored at each lead time}{lst:ch14-forecast}

\transcript{ch14-ensemble}

\noindent
The \emph{lead} is how far ahead the forecast looks, in time units. For the
first unit or two the spread is small and the ensemble mean is close to the
truth; by six the spread has stopped growing, because the members have
forgotten their start. The single run from the analysis, in the last column,
is as good as the mean at first and much worse later. Here is site 0 for
every member, drawn against the truth:

\plotfig{ch14-plume}{The twenty members of the forecast above at site 0,
with the truth in amber. They move almost as one for nearly three time
units, then fan out; by five they are scattered across the weather's whole
range.}{an ensemble plume fanning out}

\begin{think}
In a real forecast you cannot see the truth, so you cannot compute the two
error columns. Look at the table: could the spread, which you can compute,
tell you how large the error of the mean is?
\end{think}
\reveal{Yes, roughly. Averaged over \val{ch14.cases} forecasts from different
days, the spread and the error of the mean are \val{ch14.sp.one} and
\val{ch14.err.one} at a lead of one time unit, and \val{ch14.sp.three} and
\val{ch14.err.three} at three.}
The truth is, as far as anyone can tell, one more member: the analysis
differs from it by an error of the same size as the members' nudges. So it
sits about as far from the ensemble mean as they do, and the width of the
plume measures the size of the mistake without anyone seeing the truth.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A forecast is a single number.}} A single number is the
least a forecast can tell you. The ensemble gives a number and a warning
with it: on a day the members agree, trust the mean; on a day they scatter,
the same number deserves a shrug. The width of the plume changes from day to
day with the weather itself, and it says how far ahead the forecast is worth
reading.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
A weather centre does this every day and closes the loop: today's analysis
is made by blending new measurements with yesterday's forecast.

\mermaidfig{ch14-forecast-cycle}{The forecast cycle. An inexact analysis
becomes many nudged copies; their spread and their counts become the
forecast; tomorrow the forecast meets new measurements and becomes the next
analysis.}{analysis, ensemble, forecast}

\section{A chance of rain}
\label{sec:ch14-rain}
\index{probability forecast}\index{reliability}

\noindent
Now the seventy per cent. Call site 0 your town, and say it rains there
whenever its number is above \val{ch14.rain}. Lorenz's numbers are not rain;
this is a rule for turning a number into weather, and nothing below depends
on the choice. In a long run it rains on \val{ch14.clim.rain} per cent of
occasions: that is the climate of your town, measured in the last section.

\begin{think}
At some lead time, $15$ of the \val{ch14.members} members show rain at your
town. What is the forecast chance of rain?
\end{think}
\reveal{$75$ per cent: $15$ out of $20$ is three quarters.}
That is all a chance of rain is: the fraction of equally plausible futures in
which it rains. Here it is for the forecast you have been following.

\pyregion{code/ch14/chance_of_rain.py}{chance}{A chance of rain is a count of members}{lst:ch14-chance}

\needspace{14\baselineskip}
\transcript{ch14-rain}

\noindent
For the first few time units every member says dry, and it stays dry. Then
they split. At one lead three members in four say rain, and it rains; later
only one in four does, and it rains then too. By the end the chances hover
near the climate of your town: the forecast has forgotten its start.

\begin{lab}{l14_01_chance}{A chance of rain, and keeping score}
Write \api{chance}, which turns a list of members into a probability, and
\api{hit\_rate}, which scores many forecasts at once: of those that gave a
chance within a range, what fraction were followed by rain? One test runs
the chapter's own forecast and expects your \api{chance} to agree with the
table above.
\end{lab}

\begin{think}
Near the end of the table the ensemble gave rain one chance in four, and it
rained. Was the forecast wrong?
\end{think}
\reveal{You cannot tell from one day. A chance of one in four says that on
about a quarter of such days it rains, and this may be one of them.}

\needspace{9\baselineskip}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A 30 per cent chance of rain, followed by rain, was a wrong
forecast.}} A good forecast of that kind comes true about three times in
ten, and this was one of them. What would make it wrong is a record: if,
over hundreds of forecasts of $30$ per cent, it rained on $10$ per cent of
them, or $60$. Forecasters call the check \emph{reliability}, and it can only
be made over many forecasts, never over one.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
So keep score. The chapter's measurement makes \val{ch14.rel.pairs}
forecasts, at every site on many different days, each two time units ahead.
Of the \val{ch14.rel.low.n} occasions on which the ensemble gave rain a chance
between $25$ and $35$ per cent, it rained on \val{ch14.rel.low} per cent. Of
the \val{ch14.rel.high.n} on which it gave between $65$ and $75$ per cent, it
rained on \val{ch14.rel.high} per cent. The percentages mean what they
say.

\begin{worldbox}
Every day the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, based in
Reading, runs an ensemble of about fifty members of its global model, each
from a slightly different analysis, out to fifteen days ahead. Weather
services around the world turn ensembles like it, often after a statistical
correction, into the percentages on your phone and into warnings of storms
and floods. Ensembles have been run every day for more than thirty years,
and the chance of rain on your phone is, behind the scenes, a count much
like the one in the listing above.
\end{worldbox}

\section{How far ahead?}
\label{sec:ch14-horizon}
\index{forecast skill}\index{climatology}

\noindent
One forecast needs no model at all: always quote the climate. Tomorrow, at
any site, the number will be about \val{ch14.clim.average}, give or take
\val{ch14.clim.swing} \dash{} the long-run average and typical swing that the
last section measures. That forecast is never good, but its typical error is
never worse than the swing. A forecast has \emph{skill} only while it beats
it.

\begin{think}
Long after the horizon, the single run from the analysis is one more day of
weather with no connection to the truth. Which is then the better forecast:
that run, or the climate average?
\end{think}
\reveal{The climate average. Two unrelated days of weather differ by more
than either differs from the average: by about $\sqrt{2}$, that is
\num{1.4}, times the swing.}

\plotfig{ch14-skill}{Spread and errors against lead time, each averaged over
forty forecasts. The spread (dashed) tracks the error of the ensemble mean;
the single run ends well above the dotted line, the error of always quoting
the average.}{spread and error against lead time}

\noindent
Measured, the single run's error settles at \val{ch14.sat.one}, and it
becomes worse than the climate average at a lead of \val{ch14.horizon} time
units \dash{} in Lorenz's scaling, about a fortnight. A forecast that is
confidently wrong is worse than one that knows nothing. The ensemble mean
never crosses that line: as the members forget their start it drifts towards
the climate average itself, and its error settles at \val{ch14.sat.mean}, a
shade above the swing because twenty members are not infinitely many.

\begin{lab}{l14_02_saturation}{When does the ensemble stop spreading?}
Write \api{spread} for a whole ensemble, then \api{saturation\_lead}: the
first lead at which the spread reaches nine tenths of the level where it
settles, which averaged over forty forecasts is \val{ch14.sat.spread}. The
test runs the chapter's forecast through your code. One forecast is one roll
of the dice, so expect your lead to differ from the forty-forecast average,
\val{ch14.saturate} time units.
\end{lab}

\begin{think}
Suppose the analysis were ten times better, right to within a hundredth
instead of a tenth. Using the rule of Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov}, roughly how
much later would the single run lose to the climate?
\end{think}
\reveal{About $\ln 10$ divided by the exponent later, whatever the starting
error: $\ln 10 \approx \num{2.3}$, and divided by \val{ch14.lambda} that is
\val{ch14.law.step} time units.}
Measured, the horizon moves from \val{ch14.horizon} to \val{ch14.law.two}, and
with an analysis ten times better again, to \val{ch14.law.three}: gains of
\val{ch14.law.gain.a} and \val{ch14.law.gain.b}, close to the rule. Each
tenfold improvement buys the same extra stretch, never ten times as much.

The real atmosphere is less generous than the toy, and Lorenz explained why
in 1969. It has motions on many scales, from the width of an ocean down to a
gust; the small ones go wrong fastest, and their errors spread upwards into
the large ones. Better measurements delay the damage but do not stop it. His
conclusion, still the commonly quoted figure, is that detailed weather
cannot be predicted more than about two weeks ahead, however good the data.
Forecasts have improved steadily all the same \dash{} better observations,
not least from satellites, better models, bigger computers \dash{} but the
gain is usually put at about one day of useful lead time per decade: the
logarithm in the rule of Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov}, paid for in decades.

\begin{rigourbox}
The two-week figure is not derived in this book, and the toy's horizon, which
moves whenever its analysis error does, is not a measurement of it. The
figure comes from estimates of how fast errors grow at each scale of the
real atmosphere, begun by Lorenz in 1969 and refined since by comparing
forecasts with what then happened. The toy shows why there is a horizon.
\end{rigourbox}

\section{Weather and climate}
\label{sec:ch14-climate}
\index{climate}\index{climate projection}

\noindent
If nobody can say whether it will rain in your town three weeks from now,
can anybody say how often it will rain there next year?

\begin{think}
Run the model twice from different starts, each for $500$ time units, a few
years of Lorenz's weather. After the first few units their weather has
nothing in common. Will their long-run averages, their typical swings and
how often it rains differ too?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. Both runs give an average of \val{ch14.clim.average}, a swing of
\val{ch14.clim.swing} and rain on \val{ch14.clim.rain} per cent of occasions
to the figures printed, while on their last step site 0 reads completely
different numbers.}

\pyregion{code/ch14/climate.py}{climate}{Boiling a long run down to three statistics}{lst:ch14-climate}

\transcript{ch14-climate}

\noindent
This is Chapter~\ref{ch:lorenz}'s discovery on a bigger model: the path is
unpredictable, the attractor is not, and how often the path visits each
region belongs to the rule rather than to the start. Weather is the path.
Climate is the statistics of the attractor. Forecasting the weather is an
\emph{initial-value} problem, whose answer depends on where you start and
which chaos gives a horizon. The climate does not depend on the start at all.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{If the weather cannot be predicted, the climate cannot be
predicted either.}} They answer different questions. Chaos limits questions
about the path: will it rain on this day? Questions about the statistics
\dash{} how often does it rain, how widely does the weather swing \dash{} have
stable answers, and the two runs above disagree about every day while
agreeing about the climate.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
What does change the climate is the rule. The third row of the transcript
turns the forcing up from \val{ch14.forcing} to \val{ch14.hot}, more sunshine
in the toy, and the statistics move: the average becomes
\val{ch14.hot.average}, the swing \val{ch14.hot.swing}, and it rains on
\val{ch14.hot.rain} per cent of occasions instead of \val{ch14.clim.rain}.
The weather on any single day is exactly as unpredictable as before.

\plotfig{ch14-climate}{How often each value occurs over a long run. The two
runs with forcing $8$, from different starts, lie on top of each other; with
forcing $10$ the distribution widens and more of it lies above the rain line
at $5$.}{climate statistics under two forcings}

\noindent
That is the shape of a climate projection. A climate model is run not from
today's weather but under a changed forcing \dash{} in the real case, a
changed composition of the atmosphere \dash{} and asked how the statistics
move. It is called a \emph{projection} rather than a prediction because it
is conditional on the forcing assumed. The forcing is a \emph{boundary
condition}: it belongs to the rule, not to today's state, so the weather's
horizon does not rule the projection out. Whether a particular climate model
gets the shift right is a different question, answered by testing it against
past climates, which this toy cannot settle. What it shows is the logic: an
initial condition decides the weather, a boundary condition the climate.

\needspace{24\baselineskip}
\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item Lorenz's 1996 \textbf{toy atmosphere} puts forty numbers round a circle,
  pushed by their neighbours, damped, and driven by a \textbf{forcing}. Its
  calm state is unstable, and its errors double in about \val{ch14.double}
  time units.
\item An \textbf{ensemble} is many forecasts from nudged copies of the
  \textbf{analysis}. Its \textbf{spread} grows with lead time, levels off, and
  tracks the error of the ensemble mean without anyone seeing the truth.
\item A \textbf{chance of rain} is the fraction of members in which it rains.
  A probability forecast is judged by \textbf{reliability}, over many
  forecasts, never over one day.
\item A forecast has \textbf{skill} while it beats the climate average. A
  single run loses to it at the \textbf{horizon} and ends about $\sqrt{2}$
  times worse; each tenfold better analysis moves the horizon by the same
  amount, and the commonly quoted limit for real weather is about two weeks.
\item \textbf{Weather} is the path and depends on the start; \textbf{climate}
  is the statistics of the attractor and does not. A change of forcing, a
  \textbf{boundary condition}, moves the statistics, which is what a
  \textbf{climate projection} asks about.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{checkpoint}
\item What is an analysis, and why does a forecast from it go wrong even when
  the model is perfect?
  \answerto{It is the best estimate of the present state, built from
  measurements that are never exact. Its error grows, as in any chaotic
  system, until the forecast has forgotten the start.}
\item An ensemble of $50$ members has $12$ showing rain. What is the chance
  of rain?
  \answerto{$24$ per cent: $12$ out of $50$.}
\item Over a year a forecaster said \enquote{$80$ per cent chance of rain}
  $100$ times, and it rained $55$ times. Were those forecasts reliable?
  \answerto{No. A reliable $80$ per cent is followed by rain about $80$ times
  in $100$; these forecasts were too sure of themselves.}
\item Long after the horizon, why is a single forecast worse than quoting the
  climate average, and by roughly how much?
  \answerto{By then it is one more day of weather, unrelated to the truth, and
  two unrelated days differ by about $\sqrt{2}$ times the typical swing,
  while the average is off by only the swing.}
\item In the toy atmosphere the analysis error is made a hundred times
  smaller. By about how much does the horizon move?
  \answerto{Twice \val{ch14.law.step}, about \num{2.7} time units: each tenfold
  improvement buys the same extra time.}
\item Weather or climate: will it snow in Warsaw next Christmas Day? How many
  snowy December days will Warsaw have around 2050 under a given forcing?
  \answerto{The first is a weather question, far beyond the horizon, so the
  best answer is the climate's odds. The second is a climate question about
  statistics under a stated forcing, which chaos does not rule out.}
\end{checkpoint}
