\chapter{Motion: rules of change and the map of every state}
\label{ch:motion}
\index{motion}\index{differential equation}

\noindent
The tea of Chapter~\ref{ch:models} lived in whole minutes: its rule jumped
from one minute to the next. A swinging pendulum does not jump. At every
instant it is somewhere and going somewhere, and between one instant and the
next there is always another. Newton's laws of motion, published in 1687, do
not say where a planet will be next Tuesday. They say how fast its velocity
is changing right now. How do you get a future out of a rule like that?

This chapter answers with a trick you already know from
Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration}: take small steps. It then draws the one picture
the rest of the book is drawn in \dash{} a map on which every possible state
of a system is a point, and its whole future is a path \dash{} and ends with
a theorem that says why the weather model of Chapter~\ref{ch:lorenz} needs
three numbers.

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{read a rule of change as a statement about how fast each number of
  the state is changing right now}
\outcome{turn a rule of change into a path in code, with Euler's method and
  with RK4}
\outcome{use energy to check whether a simulation is telling the truth}
\outcome{read a phase portrait: its fixed points, its loops and which way the
  motion goes}
\outcome{say what motion in a plane can and cannot do, and how many numbers
  chaos in continuous time needs}
\end{outcomes}

\section{A rule that says how fast}
\label{sec:ch04-rates}
\index{rule of change}\index{velocity}

\noindent
A car's speedometer is a rule of change. It does not tell you where you are;
it tells you how fast where you are is changing, right now.

\begin{think}
The speedometer reads $60$ kilometres an hour. Roughly where will the car be
in one minute? And in one hour?
\end{think}
\reveal{About one kilometre further on in a minute, because $60$ kilometres
an hour is a kilometre a minute. In an hour you cannot say: the speed will
have changed many times, and the reading was only true at one instant.}
That is the whole difficulty, and the whole way out. Over a short enough
time, how fast something is changing tells you how far it goes: distance is
speed times time. Over a long time it tells you little, because the speed
changes. So you go a short way, look at the speedometer again, and go on.

Here is the first worked system. A weight hangs on a spring; you pull it one
metre from where it rests and let go. Its state is two numbers: its
\emph{position} $x$, how far it is from rest in metres, and its
\emph{velocity} $v$, how fast $x$ is changing in metres per second. The rule
says how fast each of them is changing:
\begin{itemize}
\item the position changes at the rate $v$ \dash{} that is what a velocity
  is;
\item the velocity changes at the rate $-k\,x$: the spring pulls back towards
  the middle, harder the further it is stretched. The number $k$ is how stiff
  the spring is; take $k = 1$. The minus sign says the pull is backwards:
  stretched to the right, the spring pulls left.
\end{itemize}
In other books you will meet the same rule written as
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = v, \qquad
   \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = -k\,x, \]
where $\tfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}$ is read \enquote{how fast $x$ is
changing}. A rule of this kind is a \emph{differential equation}.
Mathematicians spend whole courses solving them by hand. This book never
does. It follows them in small steps, which is also how a computer
forecasting the weather or steering a spacecraft does it.

\section{Small steps}
\label{sec:ch04-euler}
\index{Euler's method}\index{time step}

\noindent
The simplest way to follow a rule of change is named after Leonhard Euler,
who described it in the eighteenth century. Pick a short length of time, the
\emph{step}, and move each number for one step at the rate it has now:
\[ \text{new } x = x + \text{step} \times v, \qquad
   \text{new } v = v + \text{step} \times (-k\,x). \]
Then ask the rule again at the new state, and repeat. A rule of change has
become a rule of the next step, and the iteration of
Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration} takes over.

\mermaidfig{ch04-step-loop}{Euler's method. The rule gives the rates at this
instant; a small step moves as if they stayed the same; at the new state the
rule is asked again.}{a rule of change, followed in steps}

\pyregion{code/ch04/spring.py}{euler}{A spring, followed with Euler's method}{lst:ch04-euler}

\noindent
The function \code{rates} is the rule; \code{euler\_step} is Euler's method,
two lines; \code{energy} adds the energy of motion, half of $v \times v$, to
the energy stored in the spring, half of $k \times x \times x$. The program
starts the weight at $x = 1$, at rest, takes steps of \val{ch04.euler.dt}
seconds and prints every two seconds.

\begin{think}
A real spring with no friction swings back and forth for ever, reaching the
same distance each time, because nothing takes energy out and nothing puts
energy in. What should the energy column show, line after line?
\end{think}
\reveal{The same number every time: \num{0.5}, half of $1 \times 1$, which is
what it was at the start.}

\transcript{ch04-spring-euler}

\noindent
It does not. The weight swings further each time and the energy climbs,
from \num{0.5} to \val{ch04.euler.energy.ten} in ten seconds. Nothing in the
rule put that energy in. The simulation did.

The reason is a picture. Plot the state as a point, position across and
velocity up. With $k = 1$ the energy is half the square of the point's
distance from the centre, so the real spring, whose energy never changes,
goes round and round a circle. An Euler step moves along the straight line
the rule points along at that instant, and a straight line that touches a
circle leaves it on the outside. Every step lands a little outside, and the
next starts from there. The step is at right angles to the line from the
centre, so Pythagoras makes it exact: each step multiplies the energy by
$1 + \text{step} \times \text{step}$, which is \val{ch04.euler.factor} for a
step of \val{ch04.euler.dt}. One per cent a step, \val{ch04.euler.steps}
times in ten seconds.

\begin{think}
Take steps ten times smaller, \val{ch04.small.dt} seconds. Is the simulation
now right?
\end{think}
\reveal{It is right for longer, not right. Over the same ten seconds the
energy gains \val{ch04.small.gain.ten} per cent instead of
\val{ch04.euler.gain.ten}, but the gain never stops: after a hundred seconds
it has gained \val{ch04.small.gain.hundred} per cent, about what the larger
step gained in ten.}
Each small step gains a hundred times less than a large one, and there are
ten times as many of them in every second, so the damage per second is ten
times smaller. Ten times smaller steps bought ten times as long, for ten
times the work.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A smaller step makes the simulation right.}} It makes it
right for longer. The error of a stepping method is not a fixed amount that a
small enough step removes; it is made fresh at every step and it piles up,
so a smaller step buys time before the pile grows too large. Every
simulation has such a horizon, and the honest question to ask of one is not
how small its step was but how long it has been checked for. Part~II of this
book meets a second, harsher horizon, one that no step size can move.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Look ahead, and average}
\label{sec:ch04-rk4}
\index{Runge--Kutta method}\index{RK4}

\noindent
Euler's trouble is that it asks the rule once, at the start of the step, and
trusts the answer for the whole step. The rates change during the step, and
Euler never finds out. A better stepper asks more than once. The one this
book uses was worked out by Carl Runge and Martin Kutta at the turn of the
twentieth century and is known as RK4. It is a recipe of four samples:
\begin{enumerate}
\item ask the rule for the rates at the start;
\item take half a step at those rates, and ask again;
\item go back, take half a step at the second rates, and ask again;
\item go back, take a whole step at the third rates, and ask a fourth time.
\end{enumerate}
Then take the real step at an average of the four, counting the two middle
samples twice. That is the whole method:

\pyregion{code/src/chaoslab/flows.py}{rk4}{The RK4 recipe, from the book's library}{lst:ch04-rk4}

\noindent
\code{field} is the rule of change and \code{k1} to \code{k4} are the four
samples; \code{\_add(state, k1, dt / 2)} means \enquote{where you would be
after half a step at the rates \code{k1}}. The weights $1$, $2$, $2$, $1$
are not a guess: they are chosen so that the errors of the four samples
cancel as far as they can, which is a page of algebra this book skips.

\begin{projectbox}
\code{chaoslab}'s \code{flows.py} holds three functions:
\api{euler\_step}, \api{rk4\_step} and \api{integrate}, which calls a stepper
over and over and keeps every state. A rule of change is any function that
takes the state as a tuple of numbers and returns their rates. The same three
functions carry the three-number weather model of Chapter~\ref{ch:lorenz}
and the two pendulums of Chapter~\ref{ch:mechanics}.
\end{projectbox}

\begin{lab}{l04_01_rk4}{One step of RK4}

\tfrac{1}{24}$, close to the true answer, the number $e \approx

\end{lab}

\begin{think}
RK4 asks the rule four times a step and Euler once, so for the same work
Euler can take steps four times smaller. Which would you rather have: Euler
with steps of \val{ch04.same.dt} seconds, or RK4 with steps of
\val{ch04.euler.dt}?
\end{think}
\reveal{RK4, by a distance. Over ten seconds Euler with the small steps
gains \val{ch04.same.gain} per cent of its energy; RK4 loses
\val{ch04.rk4.ppm} parts in a million. For the same work its error is about
\val{ch04.rk4.better} times smaller.}

\noindent

\section{The map of every state}
\label{sec:ch04-phase}
\index{phase space}\index{phase portrait}\index{pendulum}

\noindent
The left half of that figure is a new kind of picture. It plots nothing
against time. Each point in it is a whole state of the spring, a position and
a velocity together, and the motion is a path from point to point. A plane of
every possible state is called a \emph{phase space}, and a drawing of the
paths in it is a \emph{phase portrait}. Every chaotic system in this book is
drawn in one.

A pendulum is the richer example. Its state is two numbers: the angle from
hanging straight down, and how fast that angle is changing. Its rule of
change is the spring's with one difference. The pull back towards the bottom
is not proportional to the angle: it is largest when the pendulum sticks out
sideways and falls to nothing when it points straight down or straight up.
(The function that behaves like that is the sine, and \code{chaoslab}'s
pendulum rule calls \code{math.sin}.)

Now draw the rule as a map. At every point of the plane \dash{} every angle,
every speed \dash{} the rule gives the rates, and so the direction the state
is about to move in. Put a small arrow there. Motion is following the arrows.

\begin{think}
Somewhere on the map the arrow has no length at all: nothing is changing.
What are those states, as positions of a real pendulum? And what happens if
you nudge each one?
\end{think}
\reveal{Hanging straight down, at rest; and balanced exactly upside down, at
rest. Nudge the first and the pendulum swings gently around it; nudge the
second and it falls away. They are the two fixed points, one stable and one
unstable, in the sense of Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration}.}

\plotfig{ch04-pendulum-map}{The map of every state of a pendulum one metre
long. Grey arrows show which way the state moves at each point. The blue
loops are swings released at 30, 90 and 150 degrees; the green paths go over
the top; the dashed curve separates the two. The filled dot is hanging down
at rest; the two open circles are the same upside-down
position.}{a pendulum's phase portrait}

\noindent
Read it like a map. Near the middle the paths are closed loops: the pendulum
swings out and back, round the same loop for ever. A path that closes on
itself is a \emph{cycle}, and a cycle is perfectly predictable: go once round
and you know every later lap. Far above and below, the paths run the whole
width: the pendulum goes over the top and keeps turning. The dashed curve
between them is the one motion that creeps up to the upside-down position and
takes for ever to arrive.

\begin{think}
A bigger loop is longer, but the state also moves faster round it. Does a
pendulum released at $90$ degrees take longer to swing out and back than one
released at $10$?
\end{think}
\reveal{Longer: \val{ch04.pend.ninety} seconds against \val{ch04.pend.ten},
about \val{ch04.pend.ninety.pct} per cent more. Released at $170$ degrees,
nearly upside down, it takes \val{ch04.pend.big} seconds,
\val{ch04.pend.ratio} times as long.}

\pyregion{code/ch04/pendulum.py}{period}{Timing a pendulum's swing}{lst:ch04-period}

\transcript{ch04-pendulum}

\noindent
The school rule that a pendulum's swing time does not depend on how far it
swings is the small-swing version, and for small swings it is right: the
times settle on \val{ch04.pend.small} seconds as the swing shrinks. It is why
a precision pendulum clock swings through only a few degrees; clockmakers
call the slowing of a wider swing \emph{circular error}.

\section{Settle, cycle or run off}
\label{sec:ch04-settle}
\index{attractor}\index{limit cycle}\index{Poincaré--Bendixson theorem}

\noindent
A real pendulum has friction, and friction takes a little energy out of every
swing.

\begin{think}
On the map, what happens to the path of a pendulum with friction released at
$150$ degrees, and where does it end?
\end{think}
\reveal{It cannot close into a loop, because each swing is smaller than the
one before. It spirals inwards and ends on the filled dot: hanging straight
down, at rest.}
A state that nearby paths all run into is an \emph{attractor}. The damped
pendulum's resting point is the simplest kind. There is a second kind: a loop
that paths run into. The Dutch engineer Balthasar van der Pol wrote down the
classic example in the 1920s, for circuits built with radio valves. Its rule
is the spring's with an extra push:
\[ x \text{ changes at the rate } v, \qquad
   v \text{ changes at the rate } -x + (1 - x \times x)\, v. \]
While $x$ lies between $-1$ and $1$, the new term speeds the motion up; beyond
them it brakes it. Start near the middle, at \val{ch04.vdp.start.in}, and the
swing grows; start far out, at \val{ch04.vdp.start.out}, and it shrinks. After
\val{ch04.vdp.time} seconds both are swinging out to \val{ch04.vdp.reach.in}
and \val{ch04.vdp.reach.out}: the same loop, reached from inside and from
outside. A pendulum clock is one of these: its mechanism gives the pendulum a
small kick every swing, friction takes energy away, and the swing settles to
one size however hard you started it.

\plotfig{ch04-settle}{Two ways for motion in a plane to settle. Left: a
pendulum with friction, released at 150 degrees, spirals onto the point where
it hangs at rest. Right: van der Pol's rule from two starts, one inside and
one outside, winding onto the same loop.}{settling onto a point and onto a
loop}

\begin{think}
Why can two different paths on one of these maps never cross?
\end{think}
\reveal{At a crossing point the state would have two different futures, but
the rule gives exactly one arrow at every point. One state, one future: the
determinism of Chapter~\ref{ch:models}, drawn as a picture.}
In a plane that one fact is surprisingly strong. A path that may cross
neither itself nor any other path, and stays in a bounded region, is fenced
in by its own past. It can wind towards a fixed point or wind towards a loop,
and the only other thing it can do is run off to infinity. It cannot wander
for ever without settling.

\begin{rigourbox}
That is the \emph{Poincaré--Bendixson theorem}, named after Henri Poincaré
and the Swedish mathematician Ivar Bendixson. It needs the rule to be smooth
(changing gently from point to point), and its exact statement allows one
more ending, a chain of fixed points joined by paths, which changes nothing
here. Its proof takes several pages of a university course and is in any
textbook of dynamical systems. It is about motion in continuous time: a rule
that jumps in steps can hop over its own path, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic} finds wild behaviour in a stepping rule with one
number. And it needs a plane: in three dimensions a path can pass over and
under itself, and nothing fences it in.
\end{rigourbox}

\noindent
So chaos in continuous time needs at least three numbers of state. That is
why the weather model of Chapter~\ref{ch:lorenz}, which Edward Lorenz cut
down in 1963 from a larger model of the atmosphere, has three numbers and not
two: with two it could not do what it does.

\begin{think}
One pendulum has two numbers of state, so the theorem fences it in. Hang a
second pendulum from the bottom of the first. How many numbers is the state
now, and does the theorem still promise simple motion?
\end{think}
\reveal{Four: an angle and a speed for each pendulum. The theorem says
nothing about four numbers, so it promises nothing, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:mechanics} shows that the double pendulum's motion is
chaotic.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A pendulum is a simple system, so its motion must be
simple.}} For one pendulum it is: two numbers of state, a plane, and a
theorem that allows only settling, cycling or running off. Hang a second
pendulum from the first and the state has four numbers, the fence is gone,
and the motion can become chaotic. How simple a system looks \dash{} two
rods, two weights, one law of gravity \dash{} says little about how simple
its motion is. Counting the numbers in its state says more.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Two bodies: the clockwork at its best}
\label{sec:ch04-planets}
\index{two-body problem}\index{Kepler's third law}

\noindent
Four numbers of state make chaos possible; they do not make it happen. The
best evidence is the system that built the clockwork view in the first place:
one planet going round the sun. Newton's gravity is a rule of change. The
planet's position changes at its velocity, and its velocity changes towards
the sun by an amount that falls with the square of the distance: twice as
far, a quarter of the pull. The sun is so much heavier that we let it sit
still.

\pyregion{code/ch04/two_bodies.py}{gravity}{Newton's gravity as a rule of change}{lst:ch04-gravity}

\noindent
The state is four numbers: where the planet is, across and up, and how fast
it is going, across and up. The listing launches it sideways at
\val{ch04.kepler.orbits} different speeds, follows each orbit ten times round
with RK4, and times every return to the starting line. The \code{size} of an
orbit is half its longest width.

\transcript{ch04-two-bodies}

\noindent
Look at the second and third columns. The average of ten laps is the first
lap, to every digit printed: the planet comes back on schedule, again and
again, round an ellipse, a stretched circle. The last column is the same for
all three orbits, \val{ch04.kepler.law}: the square of the time for one lap,
divided by the cube of the size. Johannes Kepler found that rule in the
planets' positions in 1619; Newton showed in 1687 that it follows from
gravity; your laptop has found it again from the rule of change alone.

Four numbers, and nothing like chaos, because two quantities never change:
the energy, and the rate at which the line from sun to planet sweeps out area
(Kepler's second law). Each one that never changes pins the state down
further, and with two pinned the motion has no room left to be anything but
regular. Add a third body and that stops being true, which is where
Chapter~\ref{ch:mechanics} begins.

\begin{worldbox}
The tables of where every planet, large moon and many asteroids will be
\dash{} the ephemerides that spacecraft navigators steer by, published by
NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory \dash{} are made the way this listing made
its orbits: Newton's rule of change for the whole solar system, followed
forward in small steps, and checked against radar and spacecraft tracking.
When a probe arrives at a planet on time after years in flight, a rule of
change followed in small steps has made a prediction and been right.
\end{worldbox}

\noindent
That is the clockwork at its best: a rule of change, a computer taking small
steps, and a prediction that comes back on schedule to every digit it prints.
Part~II asks what happens when the rule is just as exact and the future is
not.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{rule of change} (a \textbf{differential equation}) says how
  fast each number of the state is changing right now, not what it will be
  next. A spring's state is a position and a velocity.
\item \textbf{Euler's method} moves each number for one short step at the
  rate it has now, and repeats. On a spring it gains energy every step, and a
  smaller step makes it \textbf{right for longer}, not right.
\item \textbf{RK4} samples the rates four times a step and averages them. For
  the same work its error is many thousands of times smaller, and it too
  drifts, only far more slowly. Energy is the check.
\item A \textbf{phase space} is a plane of every possible state; the rule is
  an arrow at every point and motion follows the arrows. A pendulum draws
  closed loops (\textbf{cycles}) around its stable \textbf{fixed point}, and
  big swings take longer than small ones.
\item Friction makes the path spiral into a fixed point, an
  \textbf{attractor}; a rule that pushes and brakes can make paths wind onto a
  loop. Paths never cross, so in a plane motion can only settle, cycle or run
  off (\textbf{Poincaré--Bendixson}), and chaos in continuous time needs at
  least three numbers.
\item Two bodies under gravity have four numbers of state and still trace
  ellipses on schedule for ever, as Kepler's third law says: three numbers are
  needed for chaos, not enough.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}

\canyou

\begin{checkpoint}
\item A speedometer reads $90$ kilometres an hour. Roughly how far does the
  car go in the next $20$ seconds, and why can the same reading not tell you
  where it will be in an hour?
  \answerto{About half a kilometre: $90$ kilometres an hour is \num{1.5}
  kilometres a minute, and $20$ seconds is a third of a minute. Over an hour
  the speed changes, and the reading is only true at one instant.}
\item A spring with $k = 1$ is at $x = 0$ moving at $v = 1$, so its energy is
  \num{0.5}. Take one Euler step of \num{0.1}. What are the new $x$, $v$ and
  energy?
  \answerto{$x = 0 + \num{0.1} \times 1 = \num{0.1}$ and
  $v = 1 + \num{0.1} \times 0 = 1$. The energy is $\num{0.5} + \num{0.005}
  = \num{0.505}$, one per cent more: exactly the factor
  $1 + \num{0.1} \times \num{0.1}$.}
\item Euler's method with a step of \num{0.1} gains \val{ch04.euler.gain.ten}
  per cent of the spring's energy in ten seconds. Roughly how long can a run
  with a step of \num{0.001} go before it has gained as much?
  \answerto{About a thousand seconds. A step a hundred times smaller gains a
  hundred times less per second, so the same gain takes a hundred times
  longer: right for longer, not right.}
\item Describe the four samples RK4 takes in one step, and how it combines
  them.
  \answerto{The rates at the start; the rates half a step ahead at the first
  rates; the rates half a step ahead at the second; the rates a whole step
  ahead at the third. It then moves the whole step at their average with
  weights $1$, $2$, $2$, $1$, divided by $6$.}
\item On a pendulum's phase portrait, what do a closed loop, a path running
  the whole width and a spiral each mean for the real pendulum?
  \answerto{A closed loop is swinging back and forth; a path across the whole
  width is going over the top and turning; a spiral inwards is a pendulum
  losing energy to friction and settling to hang at rest.}
\item Why can a smooth rule of change with two numbers of state never be
  chaotic, and how many numbers does chaos in continuous time need?
  \answerto{Paths in its plane can never cross, because each state has one
  future, so a bounded path can only settle to a fixed point or wind onto a
  loop (the Poincaré--Bendixson theorem). Chaos in continuous time needs at
  least three numbers of state.}
\end{checkpoint}
