\chapter{Feedback: why nothing grows forever}
\label{ch:feedback}
\index{feedback}\index{growth!exponential}

\noindent
In a warm dish with plenty of food, the bacterium \emph{E.~coli} divides in
two about every twenty minutes. Start with one; each new cell does the
same, and so does each of theirs. If nothing got in the way, how long would
it take for the colony to weigh more than the Earth?

Nothing like that happens, and this chapter is about why not. Something
always pushes back: the food runs short, the space fills, the waste builds
up. When the size of a thing changes how fast it grows, that is feedback,
and feedback bends the straight-line rules of Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration}
into curves. A curve is harder to predict than a line, and the rest of this
book is about how much harder. Here you meet the first surprise: a
population that runs out of room does not always come to a gentle stop.

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{tell positive feedback from negative feedback in a system described
  in words}
\outcome{turn \enquote{growth slows as room runs out} into a rule, and run
  it}
\outcome{test whether the effects of two pushes on a rule add up}
\outcome{find the fixed points of a curved rule and measure the slope at
  each by computing}
\outcome{predict from that slope what a population near its settled level
  will do next}
\end{outcomes}

\section{Doubling, and why it cannot last}
\label{sec:ch03-doubling}
\index{doubling}\index{bacteria}

\noindent
Doubling is the simplest growth there is: at every step, each one of
whatever you have becomes two. Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration} called growth like
this, in proportion to what is already there, exponential.

\begin{think}
Back to the bacterium, doubling every twenty minutes. Roughly how long until
the colony weighs more than the Earth: a century, a year, a month, or two
days?
\end{think}
\reveal{Under two days: \val{ch03.earth.doublings} doublings, which is
\val{ch03.earth.hours} hours. One cell weighs about a million-millionth of a
gram.}
Most people guess a year or more. The listing counts in whole numbers, so
nothing is rounded along the way:

\noindent
The line \code{cells = 2 * cells} is the whole of the growth rule. The rest
of the program prints the colony every eight hours:

\pyregion{code/ch03/doubling.py}{table}{The colony, every eight hours}{lst:ch03-table}

\transcript{ch03-doubling}

\noindent
In Python's shorthand for very large and very small numbers,
\code{4.7e+09} is \num{4.7} with the decimal point moved nine places to the
right, and \code{1.0e-12} is the point moved twelve places to the left. After
one day the colony would weigh about \val{ch03.day.tonnes} tonnes; less than
a day later, more than the planet.

Doubling fools the eye because each step adds more than all the steps before
it together. Put one grain of rice on the first square of a chessboard, two
on the second, four on the third, and so on: the first four squares hold
$1 + 2 + 4 + 8 = 15$ grains and the fifth holds $16$ on its own, and at the
end the last square holds more than the other sixty-three together.

Nothing grows like that for long. A flask of broth stops its bacteria at a
few billion cells in every millilitre, when the food is gone and the waste
has built up. Growth that feeds on something runs into the limit of that
something.

\section{Feedback}
\label{sec:ch03-feedback}
\index{feedback!positive}\index{feedback!negative}

\noindent
Look again at the doubling rule. The more bacteria there are, the more
divisions happen in the next twenty minutes: the size of the colony sets
its own rate of growth. A quantity whose size changes its own rate of change
is under \emph{feedback}: its effect comes back round and acts on itself.

Feedback comes in two kinds. \emph{Positive feedback} pushes further in the
direction things are already going. Hold a microphone near the loudspeaker
it feeds and a faint sound goes round and round the loop, louder every
time, until the howl is as loud as the amplifier can make it.
\emph{Negative feedback} pushes back. A thermostat switches the heating off
when the room is too warm and on when it is too cold. Crowding is
negative feedback too: the more animals there are, the less food each one
gets and the fewer young the next generation has. Predators and prey make a
double loop: more rabbits feed more foxes, more foxes eat more rabbits, and
fewer rabbits starve the foxes.

\mermaidfig{ch03-crowding-loop}{Crowding as negative feedback. The more
animals there are, the fewer young each of them leaves, so the loop pulls
the population back.}{crowding as a negative feedback loop}

\begin{think}
A rumour says a bank may fail, and some of its customers take their money
out. Does that push the bank towards failing or away from it \dash{} and so,
is it positive or negative feedback?
\end{think}
\reveal{Positive. Each withdrawal leaves the bank weaker, which makes the
next customer more likely to withdraw too. The run feeds on itself.}
The run on Northern Rock in September 2007, the first on a British bank for
well over a century, ended only when the government guaranteed the deposits:
negative feedback, added from outside. Positive feedback ends like that, or
at a limit, and the limit is where the interesting mathematics starts.

\section{Growth slows as room runs out}
\label{sec:ch03-logistic}
\index{logistic growth}\index{Verhulst, Pierre-François}\index{S-curve}

\noindent
Here is the simplest way to write crowding into a rule. Measure the
population not in animals but as a fraction of the most the place could
ever hold. Call it $x$: $x = 0$ is an empty place and $x = 1$ is one where
every scrap of food and space is taken. Then $1 - x$ is the share of the
room still free.

With room to spare, suppose each animal leaves $r$ young that live to breed,
so the next generation is $r$ times this one: $x \to r x$. With $r = 2$ that
is the doubling rule. Now let the crowding slow it down, by multiplying by
the share of the room that is still free:
\[ x \;\to\; r\,x\,(1 - x). \]
In words: the next generation is what this one would breed with room to
spare, scaled down by the room that is left. While $x$ is tiny, $1 - x$ is
nearly $1$ and the rule is nearly the doubling rule. As $x$ grows the brake
grows with it, and at $x = 1$ the next generation is nothing. The Belgian
mathematician Pierre-François Verhulst proposed a law of growth that slows
in this way in 1838; the rule above is his idea taken one generation at a
time.

\pyregion{code/ch03/crowding.py}{rule}{The crowding rule, one generation at a time}{lst:ch03-rule}

\noindent
Run it beside plain doubling, both from a thousandth of the room, with
$r = 2$:

\pyregion{code/ch03/crowding.py}{run}{Doubling, and doubling with the room running out}{lst:ch03-run}

\transcript{ch03-crowding}

\noindent
For six generations the columns are hard to tell apart; a place a few per
cent full hardly feels crowded. Then they part. The doubling column passes
the whole room at generation \val{ch03.exp.full}, which is impossible, and
carries on regardless. The crowded column slows, settles at \num{0.5} and
stays there.

\begin{think}
Read the crowded column. In which generations does the population add the
most: while it is small, when it is about half-way to where it ends up, or
when it is nearly there?
\end{think}
\reveal{About half-way. The biggest jump is from generation
\val{ch03.fast.from} to generation \val{ch03.fast.to}, where it adds
\val{ch03.fast.gain} of the room while passing \val{ch03.fast.level}, half
the level of \num{0.5} it settles at.}
Small, the population has few animals to breed; nearly full, it has no room
to breed into. Checking every level from $0$ to $1$ in steps of a
hundred-thousandth confirms that the growth in one generation is largest at
exactly half the settled level. Plotted, the crowded column makes the
S-shaped curve of growth that meets a limit.

\plotfig{ch03-s-curve}{Doubling against doubling with the room running out,
both from a thousandth of the room. They agree while the place is nearly
empty; then one leaves the page and the other bends into an S.}{exponential
against logistic growth}

\begin{worldbox}
Fisheries science has used growth curves of this shape since the middle of
the twentieth century. A fished-down stock grows back fastest when it is
about half its unfished size, so the largest catch that can be taken year
after year without shrinking it, the \emph{maximum sustainable yield}, is
the one that holds the stock near that half. The idea is written into the
1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. It is still a model,
and real stocks have collapsed under quotas that looked safe on paper.
\end{worldbox}

\section{Two pushes that do not add up}
\label{sec:ch03-superposition}
\index{superposition}\index{nonlinear}

\noindent
Every rule in Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration} was a straight line: draw the next
value against this one and you get a straight line. Straight-line rules have
a property so natural that you probably assume it of every rule: the effects
of pushes add up. Take the doubling rule with a population of \num{0.2}. Push
in an extra \num{0.1} and the next generation rises by \num{0.2}; push in
\num{0.2} and it rises by \num{0.4}. Two pushes together do what each does
separately, added. This is called \emph{superposition}, and it is what makes
straight-line rules easy: you can take a problem to pieces, solve each piece
and add up the answers.

Now the crowding rule, with $r = 2$ and the same population of \num{0.2}.
Unpushed, the next generation is $2 \times \num{0.2} \times \num{0.8} =
\num{0.32}$. Push in \num{0.1} and it is $2 \times \num{0.3} \times \num{0.7}
= \num{0.42}$: the push raised it by \num{0.1}.

\begin{think}
Now push in \num{0.2}, two of those pushes at once. By how much does the
next generation rise? Guess first, then work out $2 \times \num{0.4} \times
\num{0.6}$.
\end{think}
\reveal{By \num{0.16}, not \num{0.2}: the next generation is \num{0.48}. The
second push was worth only \num{0.06}, because it landed on a more crowded
place than the first.}
It gets stranger higher up. From a population of \num{0.6} the next
generation is $2 \times \num{0.6} \times \num{0.4} = \num{0.48}$; push in
\num{0.1} and it is $2 \times \num{0.7} \times \num{0.3} = \num{0.42}$.
Adding animals now makes the next generation \emph{smaller}. The same push
helps near the bottom and hurts near the top: the effect of a change depends
on where you already are. That is what it means for a rule to be
\emph{nonlinear}, and it is what feedback does to a rule.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The effect of two pushes is the sum of the effects.}} Only
for a straight-line rule. On a curved one the effect of a push depends on
where the system already stands and on whatever else is pushing it, so two
causes that each do a little can together do much more, much less, or the
opposite. Every rule with feedback in it is curved.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Where it settles, and the slope}
\label{sec:ch03-slope}
\index{fixed point}\index{slope}\index{stability}

\noindent
The crowded population settled at \num{0.5}. Why there? A population stays
put when the next generation is the same size as this one. The empty place
is one such level: nothing breeds nothing. The other is where each animal
leaves, on average, exactly one young to replace itself. With the share
$1 - x$ of the room free each animal leaves $r(1 - x)$, and that is one when
$1 - x$ is $1/r$, which is at
\[ x = 1 - \frac{1}{r}. \]
For $r = 2$ that is $1 - \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, where the column
settled. A level the rule leaves unchanged is a \emph{fixed point}, as in
Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration}.

\begin{think}
With $r = \num{1.5}$, where does the population settle?
\end{think}
\reveal{At one third: $1/\num{1.5}$ is two thirds, and $1 - \tfrac{2}{3} =
\tfrac{1}{3}$.}
A fixed point is only half the answer; the other half is whether a
population near it is drawn in or pushed away. For a straight-line rule
$x \to a x + b$ that was decided by the multiplier $a$: a nudge away from
the fixed point is multiplied by $a$ at every step, so it shrinks when $a$ is
between $-1$ and $1$ and grows otherwise. A curve has no single multiplier;
its steepness changes from place to place. But zoom in on it, and near any
one point it looks straight \dash{} and a straight line does have a
multiplier. It is the line's steepness, its \emph{slope}.

\plotfig{ch03-zoom}{The crowding rule with $r = \num{1.5}$, zoomed in on its
fixed point at one third. Close enough, the curve is indistinguishable from
a straight line, and the steepness of that line decides what happens to a
small nudge.}{zooming in until a curve looks straight}

\noindent
So measure the slope: nudge the population a millionth up and a millionth
down, and see how far apart the next generations land.

\pyregion{code/ch03/settle.py}{slope}{How much one generation multiplies a tiny nudge}{lst:ch03-slope}

\pyregion{code/ch03/settle.py}{table}{The slope at both fixed points, for two growth rates}{lst:ch03-slopes}

\transcript{ch03-settle}

\noindent
At the fixed point of one third the slope is \val{ch03.slope.third}: a
small nudge is halved every generation, so the population is drawn back in
and the fixed point is \emph{stable}. At the empty place the slope is
\val{ch03.slope.zero}: a few animals in an empty place multiply, which is
growth, and that fixed point is \emph{unstable}. With $r = 2$ the slope at
one half is \val{ch03.slope.half}. That fixed point sits on the flat top of
the hump, where a nudge is all but wiped out in a single generation, which is
why the crowded column snapped onto \num{0.5} so fast.

This is the idea the rest of the book leans on: \textbf{on a curve, the
slope at a point is the multiplier of a tiny nudge there}, found by nudging
and looking.

\begin{rigourbox}
Taken on trust here: that the slope decides for a curve what the
multiplier decided for a line. It holds for nudges small enough that the
curve looks straight, which the zoom shows but does not prove; the proof is
in any first course on dynamical systems. The measurement estimates what
calculus calls the \emph{derivative}, exactly here to every digit printed.
\end{rigourbox}

\section{Overshoot}
\label{sec:ch03-overshoot}
\index{overshoot}\index{oscillation!damped}\index{delay}

\noindent
Turn the growth rate up to $r = \num{2.8}$. The settled level is
$1 - 1/\num{2.8}$, about \val{ch03.over.level}.

\begin{think}
Start from a small population, as before. Does it creep up to
\val{ch03.over.level} and stop, the way the populations with
$r = \num{1.5}$ and $r = 2$ did?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. It rises past the level to \val{ch03.over.peak} in generation
\val{ch03.over.peakgen}, falls below it to \val{ch03.over.fall}, and swings
back and forth, each swing smaller than the last, before it settles.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A limit to growth means growth stops smoothly.}} It can,
and with a gentle growth rate it does. With a strong one the population
overshoots the level its surroundings can sustain, crashes below it, and
oscillates. The limit is still there, but the approach to it is a series of
swings, and turned up further the swings need not die away at all.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
The slope says why. At the settled level with $r = \num{2.8}$ it is
\val{ch03.slope.over}. Its size is below one, so a nudge shrinks and the
level is stable. But it is negative: a population a little above the level
is sent a little below it, then a little above again. A negative slope is
overshoot built into the rule, and settling with swings that shrink is
called a \emph{damped oscillation}.

What puts the overshoot there is delay. The rule works out a whole
generation's change from how crowded the place is now, and looks again only a
generation later. At \val{ch03.over.before}, below its level, the population
sees plenty of room and breeds a big next generation; by the time those
young arrive the room has gone, and the population is past the level. You
have met this in an unfamiliar shower: turn the tap towards hot, nothing
happens, so you turn it further \dash{} and then it scalds you.

To test that explanation, take the delay away. Keep the same law of change,
but let the population look at its crowding ten times a generation: split
each generation's change into ten instalments, each worked out from the
population as it stands after the one before.

\pyregion{code/ch03/overshoot.py}{instalments}{The same law of change, paid in ten instalments}{lst:ch03-instalments}

\pyregion{code/ch03/overshoot.py}{compare}{Once a generation against ten times a generation}{lst:ch03-compare}

\transcript{ch03-overshoot}

\noindent
Both head for the same level, because the law of change is the same and is
zero at the same place, but only the once-a-generation population
overshoots. The one paid in instalments even grows faster at first, as its
young start breeding within the generation, and still slides into its level
without passing it. What makes the overshoot is not the speed of the growth
but how late the brakes act. Verhulst's own law is what the instalments
become as they are made ever smaller, and it never overshoots.

\plotfig{ch03-overshoot}{The same law of growth with $r = \num{2.8}$. Worked
out once a generation it overshoots its level and swings; worked out ten
times a generation it slides into the same level.}{the overshoot, and the
same law without delay}

\section{When the settling stops}
\label{sec:ch03-cycle}
\index{cycle}

\noindent
Turn the growth rate up once more, to $r = \num{3.1}$. The settled level is
still there: $1 - 1/\num{3.1}$ is about \val{ch03.two.level}, and a
population placed exactly on it would stay. But the slope there, measured as
before, is \val{ch03.slope.two}.

\begin{think}
What does a slope of \val{ch03.slope.two} do to a small nudge away from the
settled level, generation after generation?
\end{think}
\reveal{It flips the nudge to the other side and makes it a tenth bigger,
every generation. The nudge grows, so the settled level can no longer hold
the population.}

\pyregion{code/ch03/two_cycle.py}{nudge}{A nudge of a thousandth, generation by generation}{lst:ch03-nudge}

\pyregion{code/ch03/two_cycle.py}{run}{Where the population ends up}{lst:ch03-end}

\transcript{ch03-two-cycle}

\noindent
The nudge grows, changing sides every generation, and the population ends
up nowhere near the settled level. It swings between two values,
\val{ch03.two.low} and \val{ch03.two.high}, low one generation and high the
next, for ever. Nothing random has come in; the rule is the same single line.
The settling has stopped, and in its place is a cycle that repeats every two
generations. The population paid in instalments still settles at
\val{ch03.two.level}: take the delay away and the swinging goes with it.

Somewhere between $r = \num{2.8}$ and $r = \num{3.1}$ the settled level lost
its grip. Where exactly, and what the rule does as the growth rate is turned
up further still, is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic}. The rule is
one line long, a child could work it with a pencil, and it has not finished
surprising you.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Exponential growth}, in proportion to what is already there,
  adds more at each step than at all the steps before, and nothing real grows
  that way for long.
\item \textbf{Feedback} is a quantity changing its own rate of change.
  \textbf{Positive feedback} pushes on (a howl, a bank run) until it meets a
  limit; \textbf{negative feedback} pushes back (a thermostat, crowding).
\item Crowding written into doubling gives $x \to r x (1 - x)$, with $x$ the
  fraction of the room in use: an \textbf{S-curve}, growing fastest at half
  its settled level.
\item For a straight-line rule the effects of pushes add up
  (\textbf{superposition}); for a curved, \textbf{nonlinear} rule they do
  not, and a push can help or hurt depending on where the system stands.
\item The rule settles where each animal is exactly replaced, at the
  \textbf{fixed point} $1 - 1/r$. The \textbf{slope} there, measured by
  nudging, multiplies a small nudge; a size below one makes it stable.
\item A negative slope means \textbf{overshoot} and a \textbf{damped
  oscillation}. The cause is delay: the same law in small instalments does
  not overshoot.
\item Turned up further, the slope's size passes one and the population
  swings between two values for ever \dash{} the doorway to
  Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic}.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}
