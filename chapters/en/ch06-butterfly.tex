\chapter{The butterfly effect, measured}
\label{ch:butterfly}
\index{butterfly effect}\index{sensitive dependence on initial conditions}

\noindent
Chapter~\ref{ch:models} ended on a question it answered only for gentle
models. If the rule is exact, is the future known? For the cooling tea the
answer was nearly yes: two cups that started a degree apart grew closer
every minute, so a thermometer that was slightly wrong at the start hardly
mattered later.

This chapter asks the same question of the one-line rule from
Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic}, and measures the answer. Start the rule twice,
from two numbers that differ in the tenth decimal place \dash{} closer than
any thermometer, ruler or kitchen scale could ever tell apart \dash{} and let
both run. How long do they stay together? The answer is a count of steps,
it is the same on every computer, and it is short.

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{run one rule from two nearby starts and read the runs side by side}
\outcome{measure the gap between two runs step by step, and read it on a
  logarithmic scale}
\outcome{say what sensitive dependence on initial conditions means, in terms
  of something you can measure}
\outcome{use the doubling map to explain why a forecast runs out}
\outcome{say, for a given error in the start, roughly how many steps a
  prediction stays useful}
\outcome{tell determinism from predictability, and retell Lorenz's discovery
  accurately}
\end{outcomes}

\section{Two starts, one part in ten billion}
\label{sec:ch06-twins}
\index{logistic map}

\noindent
Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic}'s rule is $x_{n+1} = r\,x_n(1 - x_n)$. In words:
take the number $x_n$ you have after $n$ steps, multiply it by $1 - x_n$
and by the knob $r$, and the result is the next number, $x_{n+1}$. In this
chapter the knob is turned to $r = 4$, the largest value that keeps every
$x$ between $0$ and $1$.

Take two starts: \val{ch06.start}, and \num{0.2000000001}. They differ by
one ten-billionth, $10^{-10}$: a decimal point, nine zeros and a one.

\begin{think}
Run the rule from both starts. For roughly how many steps will the two runs
stay within \val{ch06.tol} of each other: about ten, about thirty, about three
thousand, or for ever?
\end{think}
\reveal{About thirty. The two runs first differ by more than \val{ch06.tol} at
step \val{ch06.twins.apart}.}
Most people guess thousands, or for ever, because a difference in the tenth
decimal place feels like no difference at all. For the tea it would have
been none. Here is the program that settles it.

\section{The gap doubles}
\label{sec:ch06-gap}
\index{exponential growth!of an error}

\noindent
In Chapter~\ref{ch:models} you watched the gap between two cups. Do the same
here. The program below runs the two starts, records the distance between
the runs after every step, and prints beside it what the gap would be if it
doubled exactly every step. First a question you can answer with
Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration}'s doubling arithmetic.

\begin{think}
If a gap doubles every step, how many steps does it take to grow a
thousandfold? And a billionfold?
\end{think}
\reveal{Ten steps for a thousandfold, since $2^{10} = 1024$. Thirty for a
billionfold, since a billion is a thousand times a thousand times a
thousand.}
From one ten-billionth to a tenth is exactly a billionfold. So if the gap
doubles each step, the twins should part at about step thirty, which is
where they did. Check it step by step:

\pyregion{code/ch06/gap.py}{gap}{The gap between the twins, beside a gap that doubles exactly}{lst:ch06-gap}

\transcript{ch06-gap}

\noindent
Up to step~30 the two columns keep pace. Step by step the measured gap is
ragged \dash{} some steps multiply it by nearly four, a few shrink it \dash{}
but over many steps it doubles, on average. Growth by the same factor every
step is the exponential growth of Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration}, the law of
compound interest. There it was your savings that grew. Here it is your
error.

This is the property the chapter is named after. A rule has
\emph{sensitive dependence on initial conditions} when it pushes nearby
starts apart, the gap growing by roughly the same factor at each step until
the two runs have nothing to do with each other. The closer the starts, the
longer that takes, but never for ever. Chapter~\ref{ch:models}'s tea
multiplied its gap by \num{0.9} every minute; the logistic map at $r = 4$
multiplies it, on average, by two. One factor smaller than one, one larger:
that is the whole difference between a model that forgives you and one that
does not.

\begin{think}
The doubling column goes on growing after step~35, past one and past a
hundred. Why does the measured gap stop following it?
\end{think}
\reveal{Every $x$ lies between $0$ and $1$, so two of them can never be more
than $1$ apart. Once the gap is the size of the whole range it can grow no
further: it wanders, averaging about \val{ch06.ceiling}.}
On a logarithmic scale, where each step up is a factor of ten, the story is
a straight line and a ceiling. Set it beside the tea's picture in
Chapter~\ref{ch:models}: there the straight line went down, here it goes up.

\plotfig{ch06-gap-log}{The gap between the two runs on a logarithmic scale.
It climbs, raggedly, along the dashed line of doubling until it reaches the
size of the whole range and can climb no further.}{a gap that grows, then a
ceiling}

\section{Where the doubling comes from}
\label{sec:ch06-digits}
\index{doubling map}\index{binary digits}

\noindent
Why should a gap double? For the logistic map the answer takes some algebra,
and Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov} gives it. There is a simpler rule where the
answer can be seen at a glance: the \emph{doubling map}. Double the number,
and if the result is $1$ or more, take $1$ away. It is written
$x_{n+1} = 2x_n \bmod 1$, where $\bmod 1$ means \enquote{keep only the part
after the point}.

\begin{think}
Start at \num{0.625}. Write down 1 if the number is in the upper half
(\num{0.5} or more) or 0 if it is in the lower half, then apply the doubling
map, and repeat until you have written three digits.
\end{think}
\begin{revealblock}
\num{0.625} is in the upper half: 1. Doubled it is \num{1.25}, and taking $1$
away leaves \num{0.25}, in the lower half: 0. Doubled again it is \num{0.5},
in the upper half: 1. You wrote 1, 0, 1.
\end{revealblock}
Now write \num{0.625} in \emph{binary}. In the decimals you learnt at school
the digits after the point count tenths, hundredths and thousandths. In
binary they count halves, quarters and eighths, and each digit is $0$ or
$1$. Since \num{0.625} is $\tfrac12 + \tfrac18$, in binary it is written
\num{0.101}: the three digits you have just written down.

That is no coincidence. Doubling a binary number moves every digit one place
to the left, as multiplying by ten does to decimal digits, and taking $1$
away drops the digit that has crossed the point. The first digit after the
point says which half the number is in. So the doubling map reads out the
binary digits of its start, one per step: its whole future is written, in
advance, in ever finer digits of the present. The program below checks
this for the chapter's two starts.

\pyregion{code/ch06/digits.py}{digits}{Binary digits worked out directly, and read out by the doubling map}{lst:ch06-digits}

\transcript{ch06-digits}

\noindent
For each start the digits and the doubling map's record of upper and lower
halves are the same string. The two starts share their first
\val{ch06.digits.shared} binary digits, so the map keeps them on the same side
for exactly \val{ch06.digits.shared} steps, and after that the digits that
tell them apart start to be read.

Here is sensitive dependence with nothing hidden. A measurement tells you the
first so many digits of the state; the rule uses up one digit per step; once
the measured digits are used up, the forecast depends on digits nobody
measured. Ten is a little more than $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$, so one decimal
digit carries a little more than three binary ones, and ten decimal places
carry a little over thirty. A computer, too, stores only so many binary digits of
each number; what that does to a chaotic calculation is
Chapter~\ref{ch:computers}'s subject.

\mermaidfig{ch06-digits}{Why a chaotic forecast runs out. The start is known
to some digits, the rule consumes them one step at a time, and measuring
more digits buys only a few more steps each.}{digits in, steps out}

\begin{lab}{l06_02_digits}{Digits known, steps predicted}
Write \code{shared\_digits}, which counts how many leading binary digits two

for the chapter's two starts both are \val{ch06.digits.shared}.
\end{lab}

\section{What a digit is worth}
\label{sec:ch06-worth}
\index{prediction!how long it lasts}

\noindent
The doubling map says that each correct binary digit of the start buys one
more step of forecast. The logistic map at $r = 4$ is not the doubling map,
but you have seen that its gap doubles on average, so the same bargain
should hold for it, roughly. That can be measured.

\begin{think}
The twins, whose starts agreed to ten decimal places, stayed within
\val{ch06.tol} of each other for about thirty steps. An instrument a hundred
times more precise would give you twelve decimal places. About how many
steps then: about thirty-seven, or about sixty?
\end{think}
\reveal{About thirty-seven. A hundred is about $2^{7} = 128$, so an error a
hundred times smaller needs about seven more doublings to catch up.}
Twice the digits does not buy twice the forecast. The program below checks
the arithmetic on a thousand starts spread evenly between $0$ and $1$, since
any one start can be lucky or unlucky: for each size of error it counts the
steps before the forecast is more than \val{ch06.tol} out, and averages.

\pyregion{code/ch06/worth.py}{worth}{Steps a forecast survives, averaged over a thousand starts}{lst:ch06-worth}

\transcript{ch06-worth}

\noindent
Each pair of decimal places adds about seven steps, so each one adds about
\val{ch06.worth.per}, which is the doubling map's arithmetic: a decimal digit
is worth a little more than three binary ones. Read the table the other way
round and it answers the question a forecaster asks. Measure the start to
two decimal places and the forecast lasts about \val{ch06.worth.two} steps;
measure it to ten and it lasts about \val{ch06.worth.ten}; heroic precision,
twelve places, buys \val{ch06.worth.twelve}. Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov} turns
this table into one number for any chaotic system and a formula for how far
ahead it can be predicted. Here it is enough that you can measure it.

\section{Lorenz's printout}
\label{sec:ch06-lorenz}
\index{Lorenz, Edward}

\noindent
In 1961 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz, at the Massachusetts Institute of
Technology, was running a small computer model of the weather. Wanting to
look at one stretch of a run again, he restarted it from the middle, typing
in the numbers from a printout of the earlier run. The printout showed three
decimal places, \num{0.506}, where the machine had held six,
\num{0.506127}. The new run followed the old one for a while, then drifted
away, until the two forecasts had nothing in common. The difference between
the starts, about one part in four thousand, was the kind of error he had
assumed would not matter.

The logistic map can replay the accident. Run it from the full number, run
it again from the full number, and run it once from the printout.

\begin{think}
Suppose Lorenz had typed all six decimal places, \num{0.506127}, the very
number the machine had held. Would his second run have matched the first?
\end{think}
\reveal{Yes, digit for digit and step for step. There is no chance in the
rule: the same start always gives the same future.}

\pyregion{code/ch06/printout.py}{printout}{Lorenz's printout, replayed on the logistic map}{lst:ch06-printout}

\transcript{ch06-printout}

\noindent
The column \code{again} is the first run repeated, and the program compares
every number of it with the first run and prints \code{True}. The column
\code{rounded} starts \num{0.000127} away and differs by more than
\val{ch06.tol} at step \val{ch06.lorenz.apart}, sooner than the twins, because
its error started out far larger.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{If I run a chaotic model again, I will get something
different.}} Not from the same start. A chaotic rule is a machine like any
other: the same input gives the same output, every digit of it, as the
\code{again} column shows. What changes the future is a changed start,
however small. If you rerun a computer model and get a different answer,
something changed \dash{} the start, the rule or the arithmetic \dash{} and
Chapter~\ref{ch:computers} is about the last of those.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
In 1972 Lorenz gave a talk whose title asked whether the flap of a
butterfly's wings in Brazil could set off a tornado in Texas. The phrase
stuck, and sensitive dependence on initial conditions has been called the
\emph{butterfly effect} ever since. His own equations, and the shape they
draw, are Chapter~\ref{ch:lorenz}'s.

\begin{think}
A tornado strikes Texas. Suppose you could rerun the atmosphere from two
weeks earlier with one butterfly's flap removed. Would the tornado be gone?
\end{think}
\reveal{Perhaps, perhaps not, and nobody could say in advance. The rerun is a
different atmosphere: it might have no tornado, a tornado somewhere else, or
one in Texas anyway.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The butterfly causes the tornado.}} Lorenz's question was
about prediction, not about power. The storm's energy comes from the
atmosphere, not from the insect. Remove the flap and the weather takes a
different path, which may or may not contain a tornado; every other flap,
gust and breath does the same, so no single one of them is the cause. In
the replay above, the three dropped digits did not make the rounded run what
it became: the rule acting on the whole start did. What the butterfly shows
is that weather two weeks away depends on details nobody can measure.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Deterministic, and still unpredictable}
\label{sec:ch06-determinism}
\index{determinism}\index{predictability}

\noindent
Two words have been doing two different jobs in this chapter, and they need
names. A rule is \emph{deterministic} when the same start always gives the
same future: no dice, no chance. A system is \emph{predictable}, over some
span, when a start you can actually measure pins down its future over that
span. The tea is both. The logistic map at $r = 4$ is deterministic \dash{}
checked above, digit for digit \dash{} and still unpredictable beyond a few
dozen steps, because no measured start has enough digits.

\begin{think}
The logistic map at $r = 4$ jumps about with no pattern the eye can see, and
nobody can forecast it for long. Is its output random?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. Every number follows exactly from the one before, and you can
compute the whole list again and get every digit back. What cannot be had is
the start, not the rule.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Chaos means random.}} Chaos is the opposite of chance. A
chaotic system obeys its rule exactly at every step; what looks like
randomness is the rule reading out, one step at a time, digits of the start
that nobody knows. A random sequence has no rule at all. A chaotic one has a
rule you cannot outrun. How to tell the two apart when all you have is data
is Chapter~\ref{ch:life}'s question.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{worldbox}
A coin toss is used to settle things because nobody can predict it, and yet
a spinning coin obeys Newton's laws like a stone. The statistician Persi
Diaconis and colleagues had a machine built that launches a coin the same
way every time, and it lands the same way up every time. A thumb cannot
repeat its launch that closely, so the outcome of a real toss depends on
differences too small to control. The coin is deterministic, and in your
hand it is unpredictable.
\end{worldbox}

\noindent
Laplace's demon, from Chapter~\ref{ch:models}, needed the rule and the
present exactly. This chapter has priced the word \emph{exactly}. For the
logistic map every step forward costs one more binary digit of the present,
so seeing for ever needs infinitely many, and an instrument that is merely
excellent sees a few dozen steps and then nothing. The clockwork is intact.
It is the clock face that cannot be read finely enough.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item Two runs of the logistic map at $r = 4$ that start one ten-billionth
  apart agree to a millionth for \val{ch06.twins.million} steps and differ by
  more than \val{ch06.tol} from step \val{ch06.twins.apart}: counted exactly,
  the same on every computer.
\item Their gap roughly \textbf{doubles each step}, which is exponential
  growth of the error, until it reaches the size of the whole range. That is
  \textbf{sensitive dependence on initial conditions}: a straight line up on a
  logarithmic scale, then a ceiling.
\item The \textbf{doubling map} reads out the binary digits of its start, one
  per step, so its future is written in digits of the present that no
  measurement has.
\item Each correct decimal digit of the start buys only about
  \val{ch06.worth.per} more steps of forecast: twice the digits is not twice
  the forecast.
\item Lorenz's rounded restart, replayed on the logistic map: the full start
  reruns identically, the rounded one parts company. The \textbf{butterfly
  effect} is about prediction, not about causes.
\item A rule can be \textbf{deterministic} and still \textbf{unpredictable}:
  chaos is not chance, and rerunning the same start gives the same future.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}
