\chapter{Three equations for the weather: the Lorenz attractor}
\label{ch:lorenz}
\index{Lorenz system}\index{Lorenz, Edward}

\noindent
Put a wide pan of water on a gentle flame. The water at the bottom warms,
becomes lighter than the water above it, and wants to rise; the cooler water
at the top wants to sink. Past a certain heat the water stops passing the
heat quietly upwards and starts to move. It rolls, rising on one side and
sinking on the other, like a slow wheel. Air does the same over a sunny
field, and on a grander scale that rolling is one of the engines of the
weather.

Here is the question this chapter answers. Describe that roll with three
numbers and three short rules, exact, with nothing random in them. Will it
turn steadily, like a wheel? Or can three numbers be as irregular as the sky?
Part~II found chaos in rules that take one step at a time. This chapter finds
it in something that flows \dash{} and then finds, inside the chaos,
something nobody expected: a shape.

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{read Lorenz's three rules of change term by term, and say what each
  term does to the roll}
\outcome{follow the rules with RK4 and read the result from two views: one
  number against another, and one number against time}
\outcome{measure how fast two nearly identical runs separate, and turn that
  rate into a prediction horizon}
\outcome{say what an attractor is, and why a chaotic system cannot go
  anywhere it likes}
\outcome{compute a statistic of a run and check that it does not depend on
  where the run began}
\end{outcomes}

\section{Three numbers for a pan of water}
\label{sec:ch09-rules}
\index{convection}

\noindent
In 1963 Edward Lorenz, a meteorologist at the Massachusetts Institute of
Technology, published a paper called \enquote{Deterministic Nonperiodic
Flow}. Its three words are this chapter in miniature. \emph{Deterministic}:
the rules contain no chance. \emph{Nonperiodic}: the motion never repeats.
\emph{Flow}: time runs smoothly rather than in ticks, so the rule gives the
speed at which the state changes rather than its next value, as in
Chapter~\ref{ch:motion}. His model is a drastic cut-down of a fluid heated
from below, and it keeps three numbers:

\begin{itemize}
\item $x$: how fast the roll turns, and which way (positive one way,
  negative the other);
\item $y$: how much warmer the rising side is than the sinking side;
\item $z$: how far the temperature, from bottom to top, has been bent away
  from an even slope by the mixing the roll does.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Write $\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t$, read \enquote{the rate of $x$}, for how
fast $x$ is changing right now; the letters are a name for a speed, not a
fraction to simplify. Lorenz's rules are
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \sigma\,(y - x), \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = x\,(\rho - z) - y, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}t} = x\,y - \beta\,z,
\]
with three fixed numbers named by Greek letters: $\sigma$ (sigma) is $10$,
$\rho$ (rho) is $28$ and $\beta$ (beta) is $\tfrac{8}{3}$. Read them one at a
time.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The roll chases the temperature difference.} When $y$ is bigger
  than $x$, the rate of $x$ is positive and the roll speeds up; when $y$ is
  smaller, it slows. $\sigma$ says how briskly, and is a property of the
  fluid.
\item \textbf{The roll builds the difference, and the difference leaks.}
  Multiply out the second rule: $\rho x - xz - y$. A turning roll carries
  warm water up one side and cool water down the other, which builds $y$ at
  the rate $\rho x$; $\rho$ is how hard the pan is heated, measured so that
  $\rho = 1$ is the least heating that starts a roll at all. The $-y$ is the
  difference leaking away sideways. The $-xz$ is the mixing fighting back: a
  layer the roll has already stirred has less difference left to offer.
\item \textbf{The roll stirs the layer, and the layer settles.} A roll that
  carries warm water upwards ($x$ and $y$ of the same sign) bends the
  temperature profile, at the rate $xy$; left alone the profile relaxes back
  at the rate $\beta z$, where $\beta$ comes from the shape of the rolls.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Two of the terms, $xz$ and $xy$, multiply two of the three numbers together.
Every other term is a fixed number times one of them. Those two products are
the whole of the model's nonlinearity, in Chapter~\ref{ch:feedback}'s sense,
and they will turn out to be enough.

\mermaidfig{ch09-feedback}{The three numbers push one another round a loop.
The roll builds the temperature difference, the difference drives the roll,
and together they stir the layer, which weakens the difference.}{three
numbers in a loop}

\begin{think}
Suppose the water is perfectly still: $x = 0$, $y = 0$ and $z = 0$. What do
the three rules say?
\end{think}
\reveal{Every rate is zero: $10 \times (0 - 0) = 0$, then $0 - 0 = 0$, then
$0 - 0 = 0$. Nothing changes.}
That is the pan passing its heat quietly upwards with no roll at all. A state
where every rate is zero is a \emph{fixed point} of the flow, in
Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration}'s words: start there and you stay there. It is
not the only one.

\begin{think}
Try $x = y = \sqrt{72}$, which is about \val{ch09.fixed.xy}, and $z = 27$. Are
all three rates zero? You need only $\tfrac{8}{3} \times 27 = 72$.
\end{think}
\reveal{Yes. The first rate is $10 \times 0 = 0$. The second is
$x \times (28 - 27) - y = x - y = 0$. The third is $x \times x - 72 = 72 -
72 = 0$.}
This is a \emph{steady roll}: turning at a constant speed, with a constant
temperature difference and a constant amount of mixing. The same numbers with
minus signs, $x = y = -\sqrt{72}$ and $z = 27$, are the same roll turning the
other way. For any heating $\rho$ above $1$ the two steady rolls sit at
$x = y = \pm\sqrt{\beta(\rho - 1)}$ and $z = \rho - 1$, where $\pm$ means
\enquote{plus or minus}.

\begin{lab}{l09_01_rates}{Lorenz's rules, in your own hand}
Write \code{rates}, which takes the state $(x, y, z)$ and returns the three
rates, and \code{fixed\_points}, which returns every state where all three
are zero: the still pan and, when $\rho$ is above $1$, the two steady rolls.
The test checks your rates against the book's library at two settings of the
three Greek numbers, and checks that the rates really are zero at every point
you return.
\end{lab}

\begin{think}
A pendulum with friction spirals into its resting point. Lorenz's pan has
three fixed points. Started at $(1, 1, 1)$, close to the still water, will
the run settle onto one of them?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. It is pushed away from the still water, circles one steady roll
in widening loops, is flung across to circle the other, and never settles.}
At this heating neither steady roll can hold the state. They can hold it only
while $\rho$ is below $\tfrac{470}{19}$, about \val{ch09.rho.hopf}, a number
that comes out of the rules by algebra this book does not do. Turn the
heating down to $20$ and the same start does settle: the last line of the
printout is a steady roll at $x = y = -\val{ch09.steady.x}$, $z = 19$.

\transcript{ch09-butterfly}

\noindent
The time column shows the run spending its first stretch with $x$ negative,
then switching sides at moments no column gives away. The line marked
\code{loops} prints one letter per loop, \code{L} for a loop on the side where
$x$ is negative and \code{R} for the other. The run makes
\val{ch09.loops.left} loops on the left before its first on the right, and
after that the letters come in runs of anything from one to five, with no
pattern.
There is none to find: that is the \emph{nonperiodic} of Lorenz's title.

\plotfig{ch09-butterfly}{The run from $(1, 1, 1)$. Left: $x$ against $z$
from time 5 to time 60, looping round the two steady rolls (crosses) and
tracing two wings. Right: $x$ against time for the first forty time units;
the dashed lines are the steady rolls.}{the butterfly from two sides}

\noindent
Drawn as $x$ against $z$ the run traces two wings, each looping round one of
the steady rolls, and the shape has a name that needs no explaining: the
\emph{Lorenz butterfly}. Each wing is where the roll turns one way; crossing
from wing to wing is the roll reversing.

\begin{think}
Weather services today run models carrying many millions of numbers.
Lorenz's carries three. Is three enough for real chaos, or is this a sketch
of chaos that needs many more numbers to be the genuine article?
\end{think}
\reveal{Three is enough, and three is the fewest a flow can do it with.}
Chapter~\ref{ch:motion} stated the lower limit; here is the reason. In a plane a path
can never cross itself, because two paths through one point would have the
same future; so a flow with two numbers can only settle, spiral or go round a
fixed loop. With a third number the path can pass over and under itself, and
that is room enough. The next section shows the run is sensitive to its start
in exactly the way Part~II measured.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Chaos needs many variables.}} It needs three numbers and two
multiplications. Whatever makes the weather hard to forecast, it is not only
that the atmosphere is big: a pan of water cut down to three numbers is
already unpredictable. What chaos needs is a rule that pulls nearby states
apart and yet keeps them in a bounded space, which Chapter~\ref{ch:strange}
takes apart.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{worldbox}
The \emph{chaotic waterwheel} is Lorenz's pan built in wood and water. Cups
with holes in their bottoms hang round the rim of a wheel, and water pours
into the cups at the top. Willem Malkus and Louis Howard built one at MIT in
the 1970s, and its equations, after renaming the quantities, are Lorenz's:
the wheel turns one way, slows, reverses and turns the other, at moments
nobody can call in advance. The equations of a certain kind of laser turn out
to be Lorenz's too, as Hermann Haken showed in 1975.
\end{worldbox}

\section{Two starts a hair apart}
\label{sec:ch09-apart}
\index{sensitive dependence}\index{Lyapunov exponent!of a flow}

\noindent
Chapter~\ref{ch:butterfly} measured sensitivity on a map that takes one step
at a time. Here is the same measurement on a flow. The run first goes fifty
time units, to leave its start behind and be well on the butterfly; then it
is copied, and the copy is nudged by one part in a hundred million in $x$.

\pyregion{code/ch09/two_runs.py}{split}{Two runs, one part in a hundred million apart}{lst:ch09-split}

\noindent
Both are then followed with the same rule, and the program prints the $x$ of
each and their distance apart every two time units:

\transcript{ch09-two-runs}

\noindent
Up to time 14 the two runs agree to two decimals; by time 24 they are on
different wings. The distance grows about tenfold every two or three
time units, though not smoothly: it swells and shrinks as the pair goes round
a loop. It first passes $1$ at time \val{ch09.apart.first}.

Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov} gave that rate a name: the Lyapunov exponent,
written $\lambda$ (lambda), the average of the logarithm of how much a small
gap is stretched. For a map the average is per step; for a flow it is per
unit of time. The library measures it like this:

\pyregion{code/src/chaoslab/measure.py}{flowexp}{The Lyapunov exponent of a flow}{lst:ch09-flowexp}

\noindent
Follow two runs a hair apart. After each step, see how much their gap has
grown, and add up the logarithms of those growths. Then pull the second run
back to its first small distance, in the same direction, so that the gap
never grows to the size of the butterfly, where it would stop measuring
stretching. The total, divided by the time elapsed, is $\lambda$. Here it is
from three starts:

\pyregion{code/ch09/exponent.py}{exponent}{The exponent of Lorenz's flow, from three starts}{lst:ch09-exponent}

\transcript{ch09-exponent}

\noindent
Between \val{ch09.lambda.lo} and \val{ch09.lambda.hi} per time unit, from
three quite different starts: call it \val{ch09.lambda}. On average the gap
is multiplied by $e$ to the power \val{ch09.lambda}, about \val{ch09.stretch},
every time unit.

\begin{think}
Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov}'s horizon: an error grows to a tolerance in about
$\ln(\text{tolerance} / \text{error}) / \lambda$. With an error of $10^{-8}$,
a tolerance of $1$ and $\lambda = \val{ch09.lambda}$, how long should two runs
stay within $1$ of each other? You need $\ln(10^{8})$, which is about
\val{ch09.ln.eight}.
\end{think}
\reveal{About $\val{ch09.ln.eight} / \val{ch09.lambda}$, which is roughly
\val{ch09.horizon} time units. The pair above took \val{ch09.apart.first}.}
One pair is one sample. Split the run at \val{ch09.apart.pairs} different
places along the butterfly and the first passage past $1$ comes anywhere from
time \val{ch09.apart.min} to time \val{ch09.apart.max}, averaging
\val{ch09.apart.mean}. The formula predicts the average; the scatter is there
because the stretching is not the same all round the wings, and $\lambda$ is
its average.

\plotfig{ch09-apart}{Two runs that start one part in a hundred million apart.
Left: their $x$ agree, then part company. Right: their distance on a
logarithmic scale climbs along the dashed line of the exponent until it
reaches the size of the butterfly.}{two runs parting company}

\noindent
Nothing in the rule is uncertain, and yet a forecast from a start known to
eight decimal places is good for about twenty time units and then worthless.
Two runs that have drifted apart like this can be made to agree again by
letting one of them feel the other; that is Chapter~\ref{ch:taming}'s story.

\section{Unpredictable, but not anywhere}
\label{sec:ch09-attractor}
\index{attractor}

\noindent
If the future of a run cannot be forecast beyond twenty time units, how much
can be said about it at all?

\begin{think}
By time 24 the two runs above have nothing to do with each other: one has
$x$ near $-12$, the other near $8$. Let them run for a thousand time units
more. Could one of them turn up at $x = 100$, or at $z = -50$?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. Over \val{ch09.long.time} time units, $x$ never gets further
than \val{ch09.long.x} from zero either way, and $z$ stays between
\val{ch09.long.zlo} and \val{ch09.long.zhi}. That holds from wherever you
start.}
Test the second half of that answer, the \enquote{from wherever}. Take eight
starts at the corners of a box eighty wide, far outside anything in the
picture above:

\pyregion{code/ch09/cloud.py}{cloud}{Eight starts far outside the butterfly}{lst:ch09-cloud}

\noindent
Follow each for fifty time units, ignore the first five, and print the room
each run lived in:

\transcript{ch09-cloud}

\noindent
Every run is pulled in from its corner and then lives in the same room as the
long run from $(1, 1, 1)$. Notice also that the rows come in identical pairs:
$(-40, -40, -40)$ and $(40, 40, -40)$ give the same numbers. The next section
says why.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A chaotic system can go anywhere.}} Unpredictable is not the
same as unbounded. You cannot say where on the butterfly a Lorenz run will be
in a hundred time units, but you can say it will be on the butterfly, and
that is a real prediction: a narrow set of states out of all the ones the
three numbers could take.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
A set of states that runs are drawn onto, and then never leave, is called an
\emph{attractor}. The pendulum with friction has a single point for its
attractor; a clock's pendulum, kept swinging, has a loop. Lorenz's has the
butterfly, and it is the same butterfly from almost every start: the eight
corners, the still water nudged, the starts in the previous section. A run on
it never repeats and never leaves. How a path can loop for ever inside a
bounded region without crossing itself or retracing its steps is a puzzle
about the shape itself, which is why it is called a \emph{strange attractor};
Chapter~\ref{ch:strange} opens the shape up, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:fractals} measures shapes like it.

\begin{rigourbox}
Two things are shown here by running, not proved. That no run can escape can
be proved with a page of algebra about a quantity that shrinks whenever the
state is far out. That the butterfly the computer draws really exists, and
really is chaotic, rather than being an artefact of stepping and rounding,
stayed an open question for more than thirty years, until Warwick Tucker
settled it with a proof in which a computer did the arithmetic with every
rounding error accounted for.
\end{rigourbox}

\section{What you can still predict}
\label{sec:ch09-statistics}
\index{attractor!statistics of}

\noindent
The butterfly says where a run lives. The next question is how it spends its
time there.

\begin{think}
You cannot say which wing a run will be on at time 50. Can you say what
fraction of a long run it spends on the right-hand wing, where $x$ is
positive?
\end{think}
\reveal{Yes: a half. You can see why without running anything. Change the
signs of $x$ and $y$ together: the first two rates change sign with them, and
the third, $xy - \beta z$, does not change at all, because $(-x)(-y) = xy$.}
So the rules do not care which way the roll turns. Every run on one wing has
a mirror-image run on the other, which is why the cloud's rows came in pairs,
and a long run must share its time between the wings equally.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{If the path is unpredictable, nothing about it is
predictable.}} The path is unpredictable beyond about twenty time units. The
shape it lives on is not, and neither is how much of its time it spends in
each part of the shape. Those are \emph{statistics of the attractor}, and they
come out the same from every start.
\end{trapbox}

\end{lab}

\noindent
The middle column is the unpredictable part: the wing at time 50 comes out

A statistic of a finite run wobbles a little. Chop one run of
\val{ch09.long.time} time units into pieces: in pieces 100 time units long
the fraction on the right varies from piece to piece by about
\val{ch09.wobble.short}; in pieces 2000 long, by about
\val{ch09.wobble.long}; over the whole run it is \val{ch09.long.frac}. The
longer you watch, the sharper the statistic, which is the opposite of the
path, whose forecast gets worse the further ahead you look.

\plotfig{ch09-histograms}{How much of its time the run spends at each value
of $x$ and of $z$, from three starts, 500 time units each. The three outlines
lie on top of one another: the histogram does not care where the run
began.}{histograms that ignore the start}

\noindent
It is not only two averages. Draw a histogram of where the run spends its
time and the three starts give the same picture. That is the chapter's hinge
and the second big idea of this book: \textbf{the individual future of a
chaotic system is unpredictable, but where the system lives, and how often it
visits each part, is predictable}. Even $\lambda$ is a number of that kind,
the same from every start.

Chapter~\ref{ch:weather} builds on exactly this to separate a question about
next week's weather from a question about climate, and shows how forecasters
run many copies at once. Chapter~\ref{ch:computers} asks why a statistic can
be trusted even though the computer's own rounding soon makes its path a
different one from the true path.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item Lorenz's 1963 model of a pan heated from below keeps three numbers: the
  roll's speed $x$, the temperature difference $y$ and the mixing $z$. Its
  three \textbf{rules of change} each say how fast one of them changes, and
  only two terms, $xy$ and $xz$, are \textbf{nonlinear}. Its \textbf{fixed
  points} are the still pan and two \textbf{steady rolls}.
\item Followed with RK4 at $\rho = 28$, a run settles on no fixed point: it
  loops round one steady roll, then the other, switching at irregular moments
  and never repeating. Three numbers are the fewest a \textbf{flow} needs for
  chaos, and they are enough.
\item Two runs that start one part in a hundred million apart separate in
  about twenty time units. The \textbf{Lyapunov exponent} of the flow is about
  \val{ch09.lambda} per time unit, and Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov}'s horizon
  formula predicts the average separation time.
\item The runs stay inside a bounded region, drawn onto the same
  butterfly-shaped set from almost every start: an \textbf{attractor}. A
  chaotic system cannot go anywhere it likes.
\item \textbf{Statistics of the attractor}, such as the fraction of time on
  each wing and the average height, come out the same from every start, and
  sharper the longer the run. The path is unpredictable; where the system
  lives is not.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}

\item In words, what does the rule for the rate of $x$, $\sigma(y - x)$, make

  \answerto{It makes $x$ grow: the roll speeds up towards the temperature
  difference. When $y$ is smaller than $x$ the roll slows. $\sigma$ sets how
  briskly.}
\item With $\rho = 28$, where are the three fixed points of Lorenz's rules?

  \answerto{In a plane a path cannot cross itself, so it can only settle,
