\chapter{Three equations for the weather: the Lorenz attractor}
\label{ch:lorenz}
\index{Lorenz system}\index{Lorenz, Edward}

\noindent
Put a wide pan of water on a gentle flame. The warm water at the bottom
wants to rise and the cool water at the top wants to sink, and past a certain
heat the water starts to roll: up on one side, down on the other, like a
slow wheel. Air does the same over a sunny field, and on a grander scale that
rolling is one of the engines of the weather.

Describe that roll with three numbers and three short rules, exact, with
nothing random in them. Will it turn steadily, like a wheel? Or can three
numbers be as irregular as the sky? Part~II found chaos in rules that take
one step at a time. This chapter finds it in something that flows \dash{} and
then finds, inside the chaos, something nobody expected: a shape.

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{read Lorenz's three rules of change term by term, and say what each
  term does to the roll}
\outcome{follow the rules with RK4 and read the result from two views: one
  number against another, and one number against time}
\outcome{measure how fast two nearly identical runs separate, and turn that
  rate into a prediction horizon}
\outcome{say what an attractor is, and why a chaotic system cannot go
  anywhere it likes}
\outcome{compute a statistic of a run and check that it does not depend on
  where the run began}
\end{outcomes}

\section{Three numbers for a pan of water}
\label{sec:ch09-rules}
\index{convection}

\noindent
In 1963 Edward Lorenz, a meteorologist at the Massachusetts Institute of
Technology, published a paper called \enquote{Deterministic Nonperiodic
Flow}. Its title is this chapter in miniature. \emph{Deterministic}: the
rules contain no chance. \emph{Nonperiodic}: the motion never repeats.
\emph{Flow}: time runs smoothly, so the rule gives the speed at which the
state changes rather than its next value, as in Chapter~\ref{ch:motion}. His
model is a drastic cut-down of a fluid heated from below, and it keeps three
numbers:

\begin{itemize}
\item $x$: how fast the roll turns, and which way (positive one way,
  negative the other);
\item $y$: how much warmer the rising side is than the sinking side;
\item $z$: how far the temperature, from bottom to top, has been bent away
  from an even slope by the roll's mixing.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Write $\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t$, read \enquote{the rate of $x$}, for how
fast $x$ is changing right now; it is a name for a speed, not a fraction to
simplify. Lorenz's rules are
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \sigma\,(y - x), \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = x\,(\rho - z) - y, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}t} = x\,y - \beta\,z,
\]
with three fixed numbers named by Greek letters: $\sigma$ (sigma) is $10$,
$\rho$ (rho) is $28$ and $\beta$ (beta) is $\tfrac{8}{3}$. In words:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The roll chases the temperature difference.} When $y$ is bigger
  than $x$ the roll speeds up, and when it is smaller the roll slows;
  $\sigma$ says how briskly.
\item \textbf{The roll builds the difference, and the difference leaks.}
  Multiplied out, the second rule is $\rho x - xz - y$. A turning roll carries
  warm water up one side and cool water down the other, building $y$ at the
  rate $\rho x$, where $\rho$ is how hard the pan is heated ($\rho = 1$ is the
  least heating that starts a roll at all). The $-y$ is the difference leaking
  away; the $-xz$ is the mixing fighting back.
\item \textbf{The roll stirs the layer, and the layer settles.} A roll
  carrying warm water upwards ($x$ and $y$ of the same sign) bends the
  temperature profile at the rate $xy$; left alone it relaxes at the rate
  $\beta z$, where $\beta$ comes from the shape of the rolls.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Only two terms, $xz$ and $xy$, multiply two of the numbers together; every
other term is a fixed number times one of them. Those two products are the
whole of the model's nonlinearity, in Chapter~\ref{ch:feedback}'s sense, and
they will turn out to be enough.

\mermaidfig{ch09-feedback}{The three numbers push one another round a loop.
The roll builds the temperature difference, the difference drives the roll,
and together they stir the layer, which weakens the difference.}{three
numbers in a loop}

\begin{think}
Suppose the water is perfectly still: $x = 0$, $y = 0$ and $z = 0$. What do
the three rules say?
\end{think}
\reveal{Every rate is zero: $10 \times (0 - 0) = 0$, then $0 - 0 = 0$, then
$0 - 0 = 0$. Nothing changes.}
That is the pan passing its heat quietly upwards with no roll at all. A state
where every rate is zero is a \emph{fixed point} of the flow, in
Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration}'s words: start there and you stay there. It is
not the only one.

\begin{think}
Try $x = y = \sqrt{72}$, which is about \val{ch09.fixed.xy}, and $z = 27$. Are
all three rates zero? You need only $\tfrac{8}{3} \times 27 = 72$.
\end{think}
\reveal{Yes. The first rate is $10 \times 0 = 0$. The second is
$x \times (28 - 27) - y = x - y = 0$. The third is $x \times x - 72 = 72 -
72 = 0$.}
This is a \emph{steady roll}, turning at a constant speed with a constant
temperature difference. With minus signs, $x = y = -\sqrt{72}$ and $z = 27$,
it is the same roll turning the other way. For any heating $\rho$ above $1$
the two steady rolls sit at $x = y = \pm\sqrt{\beta(\rho - 1)}$ and
$z = \rho - 1$, where $\pm$ means \enquote{plus or minus}.

\begin{lab}{l09_01_rates}{Lorenz's rules, in your own hand}
Write \code{rates}, which takes the state $(x, y, z)$ and returns the three
rates, and \code{fixed\_points}, which returns every state where all three
are zero. The test checks your rates against the book's library, and checks

\end{lab}

\section{Following the flow}
\label{sec:ch09-flow}
\index{Lorenz system!butterfly}\index{RK4}

\noindent
Here are the same rules as the book's library writes them. The last three
lines are the three rules; \api{lorenz} hands back a function that takes a
state and returns its three rates, which is what your lab asked for.

\pyregion{code/src/chaoslab/systems.py}{lorenz}{Lorenz's 1963 rules in chaoslab}{lst:ch09-field}

\noindent
To follow them, use Chapter~\ref{ch:motion}'s RK4: \api{integrate} takes
steps of length \code{DT}, a hundredth of a time unit, and keeps every state.
The time unit is the model's own, not a second or an hour.

\pyregion{code/ch09/lorenz_run.py}{run}{Forty time units of Lorenz's flow, from the start (1, 1, 1)}{lst:ch09-run}

\begin{think}
A pendulum with friction spirals into its resting point. Lorenz's pan has
three fixed points. Started at $(1, 1, 1)$, close to the still water, will
the run settle onto one of them?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. It is pushed away from the still water, circles one steady roll
in widening loops, is flung across to circle the other, and never settles.}
At this heating neither steady roll can hold the state; they can only while
$\rho$ is below $\tfrac{470}{19}$, about \val{ch09.rho.hopf}, a number that
comes out of the rules by algebra this book does not do. Turn the heating
down to $20$ and the same start does settle: the last line of the printout is
a steady roll at $x = y = -\val{ch09.steady.x}$, $z = 19$.

\transcript{ch09-butterfly}

\noindent
The line marked \code{loops} prints one letter per loop: \code{L} for a loop
on the side where $x$ is negative, \code{R} for the other. The run makes
\val{ch09.loops.left} loops on the left before its first on the right, and
after that the letters come in runs of anything from one to five, with no
pattern. There is none to find: that is the \emph{nonperiodic} of Lorenz's
title.

\plotfig{ch09-butterfly}{The run from $(1, 1, 1)$. Left: $x$ against $z$
from time 5 to time 60, looping round the two steady rolls (crosses) and
tracing two wings. Right: $x$ against time for the first forty time units;
the dashed lines are the steady rolls.}{the butterfly from two sides}

\noindent
The left half of Figure~\ref{fig:ch09-butterfly} has earned the name
\emph{Lorenz butterfly}. Each wing is the roll turning one way; crossing
between them is the roll reversing.

\begin{think}
Weather services today run models carrying many millions of numbers.
Lorenz's carries three. Is three enough for real chaos, or only for a sketch
of it?
\end{think}
\reveal{Three is enough, and three is the fewest a flow can do it with.}
Chapter~\ref{ch:motion} stated the lower limit; here is the reason. In a
plane a path can never cross itself, because two paths through one point
would have the same future; so a flow with two numbers can only settle,
spiral or go round a fixed loop. With a third number the path can pass over
and under itself, and that is room enough.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Chaos needs many variables.}} It needs three numbers and two
multiplications. The weather is not hard to forecast only because the
atmosphere is big: a pan of water cut down to three numbers is already
unpredictable. What chaos does need is Chapter~\ref{ch:strange}'s subject.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{worldbox}
The \emph{chaotic waterwheel} is Lorenz's pan built in wood and water: leaky
cups hang round the rim of a wheel and water pours into the cups at the top.
Willem Malkus and Louis Howard built one at MIT, and its equations, after
renaming the quantities, are Lorenz's: the wheel turns, slows and reverses at
moments nobody can call in advance. The equations of a certain kind of laser
are Lorenz's too, as Hermann Haken showed in 1975.
\end{worldbox}

\section{Two starts a hair apart}
\label{sec:ch09-apart}
\index{sensitive dependence}\index{Lyapunov exponent!of a flow}

\noindent
Chapter~\ref{ch:butterfly} measured sensitivity on a map. Here is the same
measurement on a flow. The run first goes fifty time units, to leave its
start behind; then it is copied, and the copy is nudged by one part in a
hundred million in $x$.

\pyregion{code/ch09/two_runs.py}{split}{Two runs, one part in a hundred million apart}{lst:ch09-split}

\noindent
Both are followed with the same rule, and the program prints the $x$ of each
and their distance apart every two time units:

\transcript{ch09-two-runs}

\noindent
Up to time 14 the two runs agree to two decimals; by time 24 they are on
different wings. The distance grows about tenfold every two or three time
units, though not smoothly: it swells and shrinks as the pair goes round a
loop. It first passes $1$ at time \val{ch09.apart.first}.

Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov} named that rate the Lyapunov exponent, $\lambda$
(lambda): the average of the logarithm of how much a small gap is stretched.
For a flow the average is per unit of time. The library measures it so:

\pyregion{code/src/chaoslab/measure.py}{flowexp}{The Lyapunov exponent of a flow}{lst:ch09-flowexp}

\noindent
Follow two runs a hair apart. After each step, add up the logarithm of how
much their gap has grown, then pull the second run back to its first small
distance, in the same direction, so that the gap never grows to the size of
the butterfly, where it would stop measuring stretching. The total, divided
by the time elapsed, is $\lambda$. Here it is from three starts:

\pyregion{code/ch09/exponent.py}{exponent}{The exponent of Lorenz's flow, from three starts}{lst:ch09-exponent}

\transcript{ch09-exponent}

\noindent
Between \val{ch09.lambda.lo} and \val{ch09.lambda.hi} per time unit, from
three quite different starts: call it \val{ch09.lambda}. On average the gap
is multiplied by $e$ to the power \val{ch09.lambda}, about \val{ch09.stretch},
every time unit.

\begin{think}
Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov}'s horizon: an error grows to a tolerance in about
$\ln(\text{tolerance} / \text{error}) / \lambda$. With an error of $10^{-8}$,
a tolerance of $1$ and $\lambda = \val{ch09.lambda}$, how long should two runs
stay within $1$ of each other? You need $\ln(10^{8})$, which is about
\val{ch09.ln.eight}.
\end{think}
\reveal{About $\val{ch09.ln.eight} / \val{ch09.lambda}$, which is roughly
\val{ch09.horizon} time units. The pair above took \val{ch09.apart.first}.}
One pair is one sample. Split the run at \val{ch09.apart.pairs} different
places along the butterfly and the first passage past $1$ comes anywhere from
time \val{ch09.apart.min} to time \val{ch09.apart.max}, averaging
\val{ch09.apart.mean}. The formula predicts the average; the scatter is there
because the stretching is not the same all round the wings.

\plotfig{ch09-apart}{Two runs that start one part in a hundred million apart.
Left: their $x$ agree, then part company. Right: their distance, on a
logarithmic scale, climbs along the dashed line of the exponent until it
reaches the size of the butterfly.}{two runs parting company}

\noindent
Nothing in the rule is uncertain, and yet a forecast from a start known to
eight decimal places is good for about twenty time units. (Two runs that
have drifted apart can be made to agree again by letting one feel the other:
that is Chapter~\ref{ch:taming}'s story.)

\section{Unpredictable, but not anywhere}
\label{sec:ch09-attractor}
\index{attractor}

\noindent
If a run cannot be forecast beyond twenty time units, what can be said about
it at all?

\begin{think}
By time 24 the two runs above have nothing to do with each other: one has
$x$ near $-12$, the other near $8$. Let them run a thousand time units more.
Could one of them turn up at $x = 100$, or at $z = -50$?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. Over \val{ch09.long.time} time units, $x$ never gets further
than \val{ch09.long.x} from zero either way, and $z$ stays between
\val{ch09.long.zlo} and \val{ch09.long.zhi}. That holds from wherever you
start.}
Test the \enquote{from wherever} with eight starts at the corners of a box
eighty wide, far outside anything in the pictures above:

\pyregion{code/ch09/cloud.py}{cloud}{Eight starts far outside the butterfly}{lst:ch09-cloud}

\noindent
The program follows each for fifty time units, ignores the first five, and
prints the room each run lived in:

\transcript{ch09-cloud}

\noindent
Every run is pulled in from its corner and then lives in the same room as the
long run. Notice, too, that the rows come in identical pairs:
$(-40, -40, -40)$ and $(40, 40, -40)$ give the same numbers. The next section
says why.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A chaotic system can go anywhere.}} Unpredictable is not
unbounded. You cannot say where on the butterfly a Lorenz run will be in a
hundred time units, but you can say it will be on the butterfly, and that is
a real prediction: a thin set of states out of all the ones the three numbers
could take.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
A set of states that runs are drawn onto, and then never leave, is an
\emph{attractor}. A pendulum with friction has a single point for its
attractor; a pendulum clock, kept swinging, has a loop. Lorenz's has the
butterfly, the same one from almost every start, and a run on it never
repeats and never leaves. How a path can loop for ever in a bounded region
without crossing or retracing itself is a puzzle about the shape, which is
why it is called a \emph{strange attractor}. Chapter~\ref{ch:strange} opens
the shape up, and Chapter~\ref{ch:fractals} measures shapes like it.

\begin{rigourbox}
Two things are shown here by running, not proved. That no run can escape
can be proved with a page of algebra about a quantity that shrinks whenever
the state is far out. That the butterfly the computer draws really exists,
and really is chaotic, rather than being an artefact of stepping and
rounding, stayed open for more than thirty years, until Warwick Tucker
settled it with a proof in which a computer did the arithmetic with every
rounding error accounted for.
\end{rigourbox}

\section{What you can still predict}
\label{sec:ch09-statistics}
\index{attractor!statistics of}

\noindent
The butterfly says where a run lives. The next question is how it spends its
time there.

\begin{think}
You cannot say which wing a run will be on at time 50. Can you say what
fraction of a long run it spends on the right-hand wing, where $x$ is
positive?
\end{think}
\reveal{Yes: a half. Change the signs of $x$ and $y$ together: the first two
rates change sign with them, and the third, $xy - \beta z$, does not change
at all, because $(-x)(-y) = xy$.}
So the rules do not care which way the roll turns. Every run on one wing has
a mirror-image run on the other, which is why the cloud's rows came in pairs,
and a long run shares its time between the wings equally.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{If the path is unpredictable, nothing about it is
predictable.}} The path is unpredictable beyond about twenty time units. The
shape it lives on is not, and neither is how much of its time it spends in
each part of the shape. Those are \emph{statistics of the attractor}, and they
come out the same from every start.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{lab}{l09_02_wings}{Two numbers the start does not decide}
Write \code{fraction\_right}, the share of a run's states with $x$ above zero,
and \code{mean\_height}, the average of its $z$. The test runs Lorenz's rules
from $(1, 1, 1)$ and from a far corner of the cloud, and checks that your two
summaries agree between the runs.
\end{lab}

\noindent
The middle column is the unpredictable part: left, left, right, left, right,
and nothing you could know in advance picks it. The last two columns are the

A statistic of a finite run wobbles a little. Chop one run of
\val{ch09.long.time} time units into pieces: in pieces 100 time units long,
the fraction on the right typically strays from a half by about
\val{ch09.wobble.short}; in pieces 2000 long, by about
\val{ch09.wobble.long}; over the whole run it is \val{ch09.long.frac}. The
longer you watch, the sharper the statistic \dash{} the opposite of the path,
whose forecast gets worse the further ahead you look.

\plotfig{ch09-histograms}{How much of its time the run spends at each value

lie on top of one another: the histogram does not care where the run
began.}{histograms that ignore the start}

\noindent
The whole histogram of where the run spends its time comes out the same from
three starts. That is the chapter's hinge and the second big idea of this
book: \textbf{the individual future of a chaotic
system is unpredictable, but where the system lives, and how often it visits
each part, is predictable}. Even $\lambda$ is a number of that kind.

Chapter~\ref{ch:weather} builds on exactly this to separate a question about
next week's weather from a question about climate, and shows how forecasters
run many copies at once. Chapter~\ref{ch:computers} asks why a statistic can
be trusted even though the computer's own rounding soon makes its path a
different one from the true path.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item Lorenz's 1963 model of a heated pan keeps three numbers: the roll's
  speed $x$, the temperature difference $y$ and the mixing $z$. Only two
  terms of its three \textbf{rules of change}, $xy$ and $xz$, are
  \textbf{nonlinear}. Its \textbf{fixed points} are the still pan and two
  \textbf{steady rolls}.
\item At $\rho = 28$ a run settles on no fixed point: it loops round one
  steady roll, then the other, switching irregularly and never repeating.
  Three numbers are the fewest a \textbf{flow} needs for chaos, and enough.
\item Two runs one part in a hundred million apart separate in about twenty
  time units. The \textbf{Lyapunov exponent} of the flow is about
  \val{ch09.lambda} per time unit, and Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov}'s horizon
  formula predicts the average separation time.
\item Every run is drawn onto the same bounded, butterfly-shaped set: an
  \textbf{attractor}. A chaotic system cannot go anywhere it likes.
\item \textbf{Statistics of the attractor}, such as the fraction of time on
  each wing and the average height, are the same from every start. The path
  is unpredictable; where the system lives is not.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{checkpoint}
\item In words, what does the rule for the rate of $x$, $\sigma(y - x)$, make
  the roll do when $y$ is larger than $x$?
  \answerto{It makes $x$ grow: the roll speeds up towards the temperature
  difference.}
\item With $\rho = 28$, where are the three fixed points of Lorenz's rules?
  \answerto{The still pan, $(0, 0, 0)$, and the two steady rolls,
  $x = y = \pm\sqrt{72}$ (about \val{ch09.fixed.xy}) with $z = 27$.}
\item Why can a flow with only two numbers not be chaotic, and how many does
  Lorenz's model use?
  \answerto{In a plane a path cannot cross itself, so it can only settle,
  spiral or go round a fixed loop. Lorenz's model uses three, the fewest that
  let a path pass over and under itself.}
\item The exponent is about \val{ch09.lambda} per time unit. If the start
  were known a thousand times more precisely, how much longer would two runs
  stay within $1$ of each other?
  \answerto{About $\ln(1000) / \val{ch09.lambda}$, roughly
  \val{ch09.gain.thousand} time units more: from about twenty to about
  twenty-eight, not a thousand times as long.}
\item Someone says a Lorenz run, being chaotic, might one day reach
  $z = 200$. What do you tell them?
  \answerto{It will not: every run is drawn onto the same bounded attractor,
  where $z$ stays between about \val{ch09.long.zlo} and
  \val{ch09.long.zhi}.}
\item What fraction of a long run is spent on the right-hand wing, and how
  can you know without running anything?
  \answerto{A half. Changing the signs of $x$ and $y$ together leaves the

  \answerto{The average height $z$, between \val{ch09.height.lo} and

\end{checkpoint}
