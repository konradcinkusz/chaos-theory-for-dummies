\chapter{A number for chaos: the Lyapunov exponent}
\label{ch:lyapunov}
\index{Lyapunov exponent}\index{prediction horizon}

\noindent
Chapter~\ref{ch:butterfly} ended with a rule of thumb: at $r = 4$ the gap
between two runs of the logistic map roughly doubles at every step. It is a
good rule of thumb and a poor measurement: \enquote{roughly} hides a great
deal, and the question behind it is one you will ask of every system in this
book. How fast do small errors grow, and how far ahead does that let you see?

This chapter turns the rule of thumb into one number, the Lyapunov exponent,
and the number into a horizon: how many steps a prediction is good for. Hold
on to this question while you read: if you measured the start of a chaotic
system a thousand times more precisely, how much further ahead could you
predict it?

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{find how much one step of a rule stretches a small error, and check
  it by running two copies of a program}
\outcome{use the natural logarithm to turn a chain of multiplications into a
  sum, and back again}
\outcome{compute the Lyapunov exponent of the logistic map from an orbit, and
  read its sign}
\outcome{check a computed exponent against a value known exactly}
\outcome{turn an exponent, a measurement error and a tolerance into a
  prediction horizon}
\outcome{say what a better measurement buys, counted in steps}
\end{outcomes}

\section{One step at a time}
\label{sec:ch07-stretch}
\index{stretch}

\noindent
Start two runs of the logistic map at $r = 4$, one at $x = \num{0.3}$ and the
other $\num{1e-10}$ higher. Chapter~\ref{ch:butterfly} watched the gap
between them grow. This time, watch what each single step does to it: divide
the gap after the step by the gap before. Call that ratio the step's
\emph{stretch}. If the gap roughly doubles, the stretch is roughly $2$.

\pyregion{code/ch07/stretch.py}{rule}{The logistic map and its stretch}{lst:ch07-rule}

\noindent
The function \code{step} is the logistic map of Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic}.
The function \code{stretch} is its slope, $r(1 - 2x)$. Chapter~\ref{ch:feedback}
measured a slope by nudging $x$ and seeing how far the output moved; for this
rule it can be written down exactly, and the first lab checks the formula by
nudging. The rest of the program measures the stretch of the first eight
steps from the two runs, beside the slope where the first run is:

\pyregion{code/ch07/stretch.py}{table}{Measuring the stretch of eight steps}{lst:ch07-table}

\transcript{ch07-stretch}

\noindent
The two right-hand columns agree. Over one step a tiny gap is multiplied by
the slope of the rule where you are, so the stretch can be read off the rule
rather than measured with a second run. A negative slope also flips the gap
to the other side, which does not change its size; the table prints sizes.

And the stretches are nothing like a steady $2$. Step $3$ shrinks the gap to
less than a third of itself, and steps $4$ and $5$ nearly quadruple it. At
step $3$ the run is near $\num{0.5}$, where the parabola is flat at the top;
at steps $4$ and $5$ it is near $1$ and near $0$, where the parabola is
steep. Chaos does not stretch evenly: hard in one place, gently in another,
and sometimes it squeezes.

\begin{think}
Suppose one step multiplies a gap by $4$ and the next multiplies it by
$\tfrac14$. What has happened to the gap over the two steps? And what single
factor, used at both steps, would have done the same?
\end{think}
\reveal{Nothing has happened: $4 \times \tfrac14 = 1$, and the gap is back
where it started. The single factor is $1$, not the $\num{2.125}$ you get by
averaging $4$ and $\tfrac14$.}
Growth compounds: each step multiplies whatever the step before left behind.
So the typical stretch is not the ordinary average of the stretches. It is
the factor that, used at every step, gives the same total.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{To find the typical stretch per step, average the
stretches.}} Try it on the table. The eight stretches average
\val{ch07.eight.mean}, and a gap multiplied by that eight times would grow
about \val{ch07.eight.meanpow}-fold. Multiply the eight actual stretches
together instead and you find that the gap grew \val{ch07.eight.product}-fold.
When things multiply, adding them up answers a question nobody asked; the
average you want must multiply too.
\end{trapbox}

\section{A logarithm turns multiplying into adding}
\label{sec:ch07-log}
\index{logarithm!natural}

\noindent
Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration} used a logarithm to answer \enquote{how many steps
until?}. This chapter needs one particular logarithm, the \emph{natural}
logarithm, written $\ln$, and one thing it does. Here are a few of its values:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
\toprule
number & $\tfrac12$ & $1$ & $2$ & $4$ & $10$ & $e$ \\
\midrule
its natural logarithm & $\val{ch07.ln.half}$ & $0$ & $\val{ch07.ln.two}$ &
  $\val{ch07.ln.four}$ & $\val{ch07.ln.ten}$ & $1$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Look at $2$ and $4$. Since $4 = 2 \times 2$, its logarithm is twice that
of $2$. That is the whole of what this chapter needs:
\[ \ln(a \times b) = \ln a + \ln b. \]
In words: the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms, so
multiplying two numbers becomes adding their logarithms. Numbers above $1$
have positive logarithms and numbers between $0$ and $1$ negative ones:
$\tfrac12 \times 2 = 1$, so $\ln\tfrac12$ and $\ln 2$ add up to $\ln 1$,
which is $0$. The last column holds $e$, about \val{ch07.e}, the number whose
natural logarithm is exactly $1$; it plays the part that $10$ plays for the
logarithm to base ten. In Python the natural logarithm is \code{math.log},
and \code{math.log(2)} starts \val{ch07.ln.two.long}.

\begin{think}
Using only $\ln 2 \approx \val{ch07.ln.two}$, what is $\ln 8$? And what is
$\ln\tfrac14$?
\end{think}
\reveal{$8 = 2 \times 2 \times 2$, so $\ln 8 = \ln 2 + \ln 2 + \ln 2$, about
\val{ch07.ln.eight}. And $\tfrac14 \times 4 = 1$, so $\ln\tfrac14$ is
$-\val{ch07.ln.four}$.}
If you wrote $\num{2.07}$, you did it right: the rounding of $\ln 2$ was
counted three times.

This is how the logarithm fixes the average. If a gap is multiplied by $g$
at a step, its logarithm goes up by $\ln g$: a \emph{factor} per step becomes
a \emph{rate} per step, and rates add, so they can be averaged in the
ordinary way. For the two steps of the question, the rates $\ln 4$ and
$\ln\tfrac14$ add to $0$, the average rate is $0$, and the factor whose
logarithm is $0$ is $1$: the right answer, found by adding.

To go back from a rate to a factor you need the number whose logarithm is
that rate, written $e^{\lambda}$, \enquote{$e$ to the power $\lambda$};
$\lambda$ is the Greek letter lambda and stands for the rate. So $e^{0} = 1$,
$e^{1} = e$, and $e^{\ln 2} = 2$. Now the eight steps of the table. The
logarithms of their stretches average \val{ch07.eight.rate}, and
$e^{\val{ch07.eight.rate}}$ is about \val{ch07.eight.geo}: on average the
gap grew by a factor of about $2$ per step, and \val{ch07.eight.geo}
multiplied by itself eight times is the \val{ch07.eight.product} the table
produced. \enquote{Roughly doubles} has become a measurement.

\section{The Lyapunov exponent}
\label{sec:ch07-exponent}
\index{Lyapunov exponent!of the logistic map}

\noindent
Eight steps are a sample. Follow the orbit for a hundred thousand steps
instead, take the natural logarithm of the size of the stretch at every point
it visits, and average. The result is the \emph{Lyapunov exponent}, named
after the Russian mathematician Aleksandr Lyapunov, whose work on the
stability of motion appeared in 1892. If $s_1, s_2, \dots, s_N$ are the
stretches along the orbit,
\[ \lambda = \frac{\ln\abs{s_1} + \ln\abs{s_2} + \dots + \ln\abs{s_N}}{N}. \]
In words: $\lambda$ is the average of the logarithms of the stretches. The
bars mean \enquote{the size of}, because a flip does not make a gap bigger or
smaller.

Read $\lambda$ as a rate. Over $N$ steps a small error is multiplied by
about $e^{\lambda N}$, so its sign says what kind of system you have:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$\lambda$ above zero}: errors grow exponentially, and the system
  is chaotic;
\item \textbf{$\lambda$ below zero}: errors shrink, and the system forgets its
  disturbances;
\item \textbf{$\lambda$ equal to zero}: the borderline, where errors neither
  grow nor shrink on average.
\end{itemize}

\begin{think}
At $r = 4$ the gap roughly doubles at every step. What value of $\lambda$
would you predict?
\end{think}
\reveal{About $\ln 2$, which is \val{ch07.ln.two}: a gap multiplied by $2$
at every step grows at $\ln 2$ per step.}

\begin{think}
At $r = \num{2.8}$ the orbit settles on the fixed point $1 - 1/r$ of
Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic}. There the slope $r(1 - 2x)$ works out to
$2 - r = -\num{0.8}$, and $\ln\num{0.8}$ is about \val{ch07.ln.pointeight}.
What is $\lambda$?
\end{think}
\reveal{About \val{ch07.ln.pointeight}. Once the orbit sits on the fixed
point, every term of the average is $\ln\num{0.8}$, so the average is too.
It is negative: a nudge shrinks by a fifth at every step.}

\pyregion{code/ch07/exponent.py}{exponent}{The Lyapunov exponent, by averaging}{lst:ch07-exponent}

\noindent
The first loop runs the map for a thousand steps and throws them away, so
the orbit has settled before anything is averaged. The second adds up the
logarithm of the size of the stretch at each point, then steps; the last line
divides. That is the formula above, and nothing else. Run it at four settings
of the knob $r$:

\pyregion{code/ch07/exponent.py}{run}{The exponent at four settings of r}{lst:ch07-run}

\transcript{ch07-exponent}

\noindent
Both predictions hold. At $r = \num{2.8}$ the program finds
\val{ch07.lam.twoeight}. At $r = 4$ it finds \val{ch07.lam.four}, and the
exact value is $\ln 2 = \val{ch07.ln.two.long}$: an average of a hundred
thousand wildly different logarithms agrees with it to within $\num{0.001}$.
At $r = \num{3.2}$, where
the orbit alternates between two values, and at $r = \num{3.5}$, where it
cycles through four, the exponent is negative too, \val{ch07.lam.threetwo}
and \val{ch07.lam.threefive}. A cycle, like a fixed point, forgets its
disturbances.

\plotfig{ch07-running-average}{The running average of the logarithm of the
stretch along six orbits: three starts at $r = 4$ and three at
$r = \num{3.7}$. Each wobbles for a few hundred steps and then settles, and
the three starts at each setting settle on the same number.}{an average that
settles}

\begin{rigourbox}
Two things are used here and not proved. The first is that the average
settles on the same number from almost every start. That is a theorem, and
\enquote{almost every} matters: a start of exactly $0$ stays at $0$ for ever
and averages $\ln 4$. A computer run can illustrate the theorem, as the
figure does, but not prove it. The second is why $r = 4$
gives exactly $\ln 2$. Write $x$ as $\sin^{2}\theta$ for an angle $\theta$,
and one line of school trigonometry turns $4x(1 - x)$ into
$\sin^{2}(2\theta)$: at $r = 4$ the map is doubling an angle in disguise.
Doubling stretches every error in the angle by exactly $2$, and changing the
disguise does not change the average rate.
\end{rigourbox}

\section{How far ahead: the prediction horizon}
\label{sec:ch07-horizon}
\index{prediction horizon}\index{Lyapunov time}

\noindent
After $n$ steps a small error of size $\delta$
(the Greek letter delta) has grown to about $\delta\, e^{\lambda n}$. You
stop trusting a prediction once its error reaches a tolerance $T$, the
largest error you can live with. So the horizon is the number of steps at
which $\delta\, e^{\lambda n} = T$. Take the logarithm of both sides: the
product becomes a sum, and the logarithm of $e^{\lambda n}$ is $\lambda n$,
because $e$ has logarithm $1$ and is multiplied in $\lambda n$ times. That
leaves $\ln\delta + \lambda n = \ln T$, and so
\[ n \approx \frac{1}{\lambda}\,\ln\frac{T}{\delta}. \]
In words: the horizon is the logarithm of how many times the error is
allowed to grow, divided by the rate at which it grows.

The number $1/\lambda$ is called the \emph{Lyapunov time}:
the number of steps over which errors grow by the factor $e$. At $r = 4$ it
is $1/\ln 2$, about \val{ch07.lyap.time} steps, and the formula says that a
horizon is the Lyapunov time multiplied by $\ln(T/\delta)$.

\mermaidfig{ch07-three-steps}{From a rule to a horizon. The slope gives the
stretch at each step, the average of its logarithm gives the exponent, and
the exponent with the error and the tolerance gives the horizon.}{stretch,
exponent, horizon}

\begin{think}
At $r = 4$, with a tolerance of $T = \num{0.1}$ and an error in the start of
a millionth, the formula gives about \val{ch07.hz.million} steps. You buy an
instrument a thousand times better, so the error is a billionth. How many
steps now: a thousand times as many, twice as many, or something else?
\end{think}
\reveal{About \val{ch07.hz.billion}: ten more. A thousand times better buys
$\ln 1000/\ln 2$ extra steps, about \val{ch07.ln.thousand} divided by
\val{ch07.ln.two}, which is \val{ch07.extra.thousand}, whatever the error
was before.}

\noindent
The program sets that line beside a count. The function \code{counts},
further down the file, makes the measurement of Chapter~\ref{ch:butterfly}
from a thousand starts: two runs $\delta$ apart, followed until they differ
by more than $T$. In the output, \code{1e-03} is Python's way of writing
$10^{-3}$, a thousandth.

\noindent

\plotfig{ch07-horizon-line}{The horizon against the error in the start, on a
logarithmic scale with better measurements to the right. The count (dots,
and a band holding most of the starts) follows the formula's straight line:
every factor of ten in precision buys the same number of extra
steps.}{horizon against the error in the start}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Ten times better data gives ten times longer forecasts.}}
It gives a fixed number of extra steps, $\ln 10/\lambda$, which at $r = 4$ is
\val{ch07.per.ten}. The horizon grows with the logarithm of your precision,
not with the precision: every extra correct decimal place buys the same few
steps, the twentieth no more than the first.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{think}
A computer stores a number to about sixteen significant figures. Suppose you
could measure the start of the map at $r = 4$ that well, so that
$\delta = 10^{-16}$. With $T = \num{0.1}$, roughly how far ahead could you
predict? And how many decimal places of the start would a thousand steps
need?
\end{think}
\reveal{About \val{ch07.hz.sixteen} steps: fifteen factors of ten, at
\val{ch07.per.ten} steps each. A thousand steps would need the start to
\val{ch07.digits.thousand} decimal places; even a hundred steps needs
\val{ch07.digits.hundred}.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A bigger computer will eventually make any system
predictable.}} A faster computer does not know the start any better, and the
horizon depends on the start only through the logarithm of its error. To
double the horizon you must double the number of correct digits, and the most
precise measurements ever made, by atomic clocks, reach fewer than twenty.
The computer also rounds at every step, which is a fresh error of its own;
Chapter~\ref{ch:computers} measures what that costs.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
This is why weather forecasts improve slowly. Satellites, denser
observations and faster computers have shrunk the error in the atmosphere's
measured state many times over, and each factor bought the same fixed amount
of lead time; the improvement usually quoted is about a day of useful
forecast per decade. The atmosphere is not the logistic map, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:weather} says what is different about it, but the arithmetic
of the logarithm applies all the same.

\begin{worldbox}
The Lyapunov time is how astronomers say how far ahead an orbit can be
followed. In 1988 Gerald Sussman and Jack Wisdom tracked Pluto on a computer
built for the purpose, found its motion chaotic, and put its Lyapunov time at
about twenty million years; the planets' own story is
Chapter~\ref{ch:mechanics}'s. On its own the number is not a horizon. The
horizon is the Lyapunov time multiplied by $\ln(T/\delta)$, and that
logarithm is only about \val{ch07.ln.tenbillion} even when the error in the
start is ten billion times smaller than the tolerance, so a horizon is a few
dozen Lyapunov times at most.
\end{worldbox}

\section{Chaos coming and going}
\label{sec:ch07-sweep}
\index{Lyapunov exponent!across r}

\noindent
With one number per setting of the knob, you can turn the knob and watch
it. Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic} found a fixed point at
$r = \num{2.8}$ and a two-cycle at $r = \num{3.2}$; between them, at $r = 3$,
the fixed point gives way.

\begin{think}
At the fixed point the slope is $2 - r$. What is it at $r = 3$, and what does
that make $\lambda$?
\end{think}
\reveal{$2 - 3 = -1$, and $\ln 1 = 0$, so $\lambda = 0$: a nudge is neither
stretched nor shrunk, only flipped. Run at $r = 3$, the program finds a value
within $\num{0.001}$ of zero.}
Zero is the borderline, and it marks where one kind of behaviour hands over
to another. To find such places, take the exponent at many settings of $r$.
The listing uses the library's \api{lyapunov}, which is the
loop of \code{exponent} with the rule and its stretch passed in, so that it
works for any map:

\pyregion{code/ch07/sweep.py}{sweep}{The exponent at sixteen settings of r}{lst:ch07-sweep}

\transcript{ch07-sweep}

\noindent
Read down the middle column. It is negative up to $r = \num{3.56}$ and
positive from $r = \num{3.60}$, so chaos arrives somewhere in between. After
that it climbs towards $\ln 2$ at $r = 4$, except at $r = \num{3.84}$, where
it is negative again: a narrow range of the knob, inside the chaos, where the
orbit has found a cycle and forgets its disturbances once more. A table this
coarse steps over most such ranges; the figure does not.

\plotfig{ch07-exponent-r}{The Lyapunov exponent across the knob $r$. It is
negative wherever the orbit settles, touches zero at each hand-over, is
positive where the map is chaotic, and dips below zero again in narrow
windows. The red dots are the four values of the exponent listing.}{the
exponent against $r$}

\noindent
Below the start of chaos the curve never rises above zero. It touches zero
at $r = 3$ and again a little below $r = \num{3.5}$, each time a cycle hands
over to one twice as long. Beyond, it is mostly positive, pierced by
downward spikes: windows where order comes back. Where exactly chaos begins,
why the hand-overs crowd together and which window is widest are
Chapter~\ref{ch:bifurcations}'s questions. This chapter's answer is the
measurement that tells order from chaos at any setting: one number, and its
sign.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item One step multiplies a tiny error by the \textbf{stretch}, the slope of
  the rule where you are. Along a chaotic orbit the stretches vary wildly,
  and their ordinary average is the wrong summary.
\item The \textbf{natural logarithm} turns multiplying into adding:
  $\ln(a \times b) = \ln a + \ln b$, $\ln 1 = 0$, $\ln 2 \approx
  \val{ch07.ln.two}$ and $\ln e = 1$. A growth factor $g$ per step becomes a
  rate $\ln g$, and rates can be averaged.
\item The \textbf{Lyapunov exponent} $\lambda$ is the average of
  $\ln\abs{\text{stretch}}$ along an orbit: positive for chaos, negative for
  order, zero at the borderline. For the logistic map at $r = 4$ it is
  $\ln 2$, and the computation agrees.
\item The \textbf{prediction horizon} is about $\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln(T/\delta)$,
  and $1/\lambda$ is the \textbf{Lyapunov time}. A start measured a thousand
  times more precisely buys $\ln 1000/\lambda$ more steps, about ten at
  $r = 4$: a fixed amount, not a thousand times as many.
\item Across $r$ the exponent shows \textbf{chaos coming and going}:
  negative where the orbit settles, zero at each hand-over, positive in
  chaos, and negative again in narrow windows.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{checkpoint}
\item A gap is multiplied by $3$, then by $\tfrac13$, then by $3$, then by
  $\tfrac13$. By how much has it grown overall, and what is the ordinary
  average of the four factors?
  \answerto{It has not grown at all: the product is $1$. The ordinary
  average of the factors is $\tfrac53$, which suggests growth that never
  happened.}
\item Using $\ln 2 \approx \val{ch07.ln.two}$ and $\ln 10 \approx \val{ch07.ln.ten}$, find
  $\ln 20$ and $\ln\num{0.2}$.
  \answerto{$\ln 20 = \ln 2 + \ln 10 \approx \num{2.99}$. And
  $\num{0.2} \times 10 = 2$, so $\ln\num{0.2} = \ln 2 - \ln 10 \approx
  -\num{1.61}$.}
\item An orbit settles on a fixed point where the slope is $-\num{0.5}$. What
  is the Lyapunov exponent, and what does its sign say?
  \answerto{$\ln\num{0.5} \approx -\num{0.69}$. It is negative: errors
  shrink, halving at every step.}
\item A system has $\lambda = \num{0.5}$ per step. An error of $\num{0.001}$
  must stay below $\num{0.1}$. About how many steps is the prediction good
  for?
  \answerto{About $9$: $\ln(\num{0.1}/\num{0.001}) = \ln 100 = 2\ln 10
  \approx \num{4.6}$, divided by $\num{0.5}$.}
\item In the same system you measure the start a hundred times more
  precisely. How many steps do you gain? And if you do it again?
  \answerto{About $9$ steps each time: $\ln 100/\num{0.5} \approx \num{9.2}$.
  Each hundredfold improvement buys the same fixed number of steps.}
\item Why will a faster computer, on its own, hardly lengthen the forecast of
  a chaotic system?
  \answerto{Because the horizon depends on the error in the start only through
  its logarithm, and a faster computer does not measure the start any better.
  Doubling the horizon needs twice as many correct digits in the start.}
\item At $r = \num{3.84}$ the exponent is negative, although it is positive at
  $r = \num{3.80}$ and $r = \num{3.88}$. What is happening there?
  \answerto{A window: inside the chaotic range, the orbit has settled into a
  cycle, so errors shrink again and the exponent dips below zero.}
\end{checkpoint}
