\chapter{The road to chaos, and a number nobody expected}
\label{ch:bifurcations}
\index{bifurcation diagram}\index{Feigenbaum's constant}

\noindent
Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic} turned one knob, $r$, to four positions and found
four behaviours. At $r = \num{2.8}$ the logistic map settled on a single
value; at \num{3.2} it alternated between two; at \num{3.5} it cycled
through four; at \num{3.9} it never repeated at all. Four photographs of one
machine. This chapter asks what happens between them. Does order turn into
chaos at one setting of the knob, the way water boils at a hundred degrees?
Or gradually, along a road? And if it is a road, what does it look like?

The answer is one picture, among the most reproduced in science, and there
is a number hidden in it that nobody put there. It comes out of other
equations too, it has been measured in a laboratory with liquid helium, and
by the end of the chapter you will have computed it to five figures with
nothing but addition, subtraction, multiplication and division.

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{read a bifurcation diagram and say what the map does at any value
  of the knob it shows}
\outcome{locate a period doubling by watching runs of the map, and say what
  limits how precisely that can be done}
\outcome{find a superstable parameter by bisection, using nothing but
  $+$, $-$, $\times$ and $\div$}
\outcome{compute the ratio of successive gaps between doublings, and estimate
  where the doublings run out}
\outcome{run the same search on a second rule and say what the comparison
  shows}
\outcome{find a window of order inside the chaotic range, and state what Li
  and Yorke proved about period three}
\end{outcomes}

\section{One picture for every setting of the knob}
\label{sec:ch08-picture}
\index{bifurcation diagram}\index{transient}

\noindent
Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic} asked one question at a time: choose an $r$, run
the map, read the output. To see every $r$ at once you need a recipe that
turns a whole run into a few dots, and then you draw the dots of thousands
of runs side by side. Here is the recipe.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose a value of $r$ and start the map at $x = \num{0.5}$.
\item Run it for a thousand steps and throw them away. That stretch is the
  \emph{transient}: the orbit finding its way to wherever it is going.
\item Run it for three hundred more, and draw a dot above $r$ at the height
  of each value.
\end{enumerate}

\pyregion{code/ch08/bifurcation.py}{longrun}{The recipe: let the orbit settle, then keep what it visits}{lst:ch08-longrun}

\noindent
\code{orbit} is the book's library function that applies a rule over and
over and keeps every value, and \code{xs[burn + 1:]} keeps only the values
after the transient. Before you look at what it draws, one question about a
setting you already know.

\begin{think}
At $r = \num{2.8}$ the map settles on one value, the fixed point $1 - 1/r$
that Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic} found. Once the transient has been thrown
away, how many different dots does the recipe draw above $r = \num{2.8}$?
\end{think}
\reveal{One. All three hundred values are the same number, \val{ch08.fixed}
to four decimals, which is $1 - 1/\num{2.8}$; the dots land on top of each
other.}
A two-cycle draws two dots, a four-cycle four, and an orbit that never
repeats draws three hundred different ones, a short vertical smear. The rest
of the listing asks the question at six values of $r$ and prints what it
finds:

\transcript{ch08-bifurcation}

\noindent
Two rows are new: at \num{3.56} the orbit cycles through eight values, and
at \num{3.567} through sixteen. Now run the recipe for thousands of values
of $r$ between \num{2.8} and $4$ and draw every dot at once.

\plotfig{ch08-diagram}{The bifurcation diagram of the logistic map. Above
each value of $r$ the dots show where $x$ spends its time once the transient
is over: one line where it settles, two where it alternates, a smear where it
never repeats.}{the bifurcation diagram}

\noindent
This is the \emph{bifurcation diagram}, and it holds the whole of
Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic} in one frame. Read it from left to right, as a
journey along the knob. One line up to $r = 3$; there the line splits in
two; near \num{3.45} each branch splits again; then everything dissolves
into a smear, streaked with pale stripes. A \emph{bifurcation} (from the
Latin for a two-pronged fork) is a value of the knob at which the kind of
long-run behaviour changes. Every split you can see here is a
\emph{period doubling}: each value the orbit visited becomes two, so the
cycle takes twice as long to come round.

\begin{think}
Look at the picture between $r = \num{3.5}$, where the orbit has period
four, and the smear. Does order give way to chaos at one value of $r$, like a
switch?
\end{think}
\reveal{It looks like a switch, and it is not. Magnify the stretch just
before the smear and each branch splits again, and again: periods $8$, $16$,
$32$ and on without end. The table above has already caught two of them. All
those infinitely many splits fit in before $r = \val{ch08.end}$.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The change from order to chaos is a sudden switch.}} Not on
this road. Order breaks down in an infinite \emph{cascade} of doublings, each
arriving sooner than the one before, and chaos begins where the cascade runs
out. The smear looks sudden only because its last splits are too close
together to draw. Other systems reach chaos by other roads, but this one,
the \emph{period-doubling cascade}, is the road a single hump takes, and the
rest of the chapter measures it.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Forks that crowd together}
\label{sec:ch08-cascade}
\index{period doubling}\index{bisection}

\noindent
Where exactly is each split? The last table brackets them. The orbit has
period one at \num{2.8} and period two at \num{3.2}, so the first split lies
between. To pin it down, try the middle of the bracket, run the map, and see
which period it settles into; keep whichever half still contains the change,
and repeat. Each round halves the bracket, and twenty rounds shrink it a
millionfold. This is \emph{bisection}, and all it needs is a way to ask
\enquote{is it still period $p$ here?}

\pyregion{code/ch08/doublings.py}{fork}{Pinning down a split by halving the bracket}{lst:ch08-fork}

\noindent
\code{settled\_period} runs the map for twenty thousand steps and asks the
library's \code{period} function what repeats. The listing runs \code{fork}
on the four brackets the last table provides.

\begin{think}
The first two splits turn out to be \val{ch08.gap.one} apart in $r$, and the
next two \val{ch08.gap.two} apart. How many times smaller is the second gap
than the first? If the next gap shrinks by the same factor, how long will it
be?
\end{think}
\reveal{About \val{ch08.ratio.one} times smaller: that is
$\val{ch08.gap.one} \div \val{ch08.gap.two}$. The same factor again would
make the next gap about \num{0.02}.}
Here is what the listing measured:

\transcript{ch08-doublings}

\noindent
The third gap is \val{ch08.gap.three}, and the ratio of the second gap to
the third is \val{ch08.ratio.two}. The gaps shrink by a factor a little under
five each time. That has a consequence you can work out with the arithmetic
of Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration}. If every gap is about \num{4.7} times the
next, then all the gaps still to come add up to the last one divided by
$\num{4.7} - 1$, a little over a quarter of it. Infinitely many splits, in a
finite stretch of the knob: the cascade has an end, a short way beyond
\val{ch08.fork.four}.

The first row of the table deserves a second look.

\begin{think}
The first split is known exactly. Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic} found that the
fixed point loses its grip at $r = 3$, where the slope of the map at the
fixed point, $2 - r$, passes $-1$. The table says \val{ch08.fork.one}. What
went wrong?
\end{think}
\reveal{Nothing in the rule: the measurement ran out of patience. Just below
$3$ the fixed point is stable, but only just. At $r = \num{2.9998}$ each
step multiplies a small nudge by $2 - r = \num{-0.9998}$, so the nudge loses
a fiftieth of a per cent per step, and after twenty thousand steps the orbit
is still visibly flipping from side to side. \code{settled\_period} calls it
a two-cycle.}
Every split measured by watching comes out early for the same reason: close
to a split, the cycle that is about to lose its grip settles very slowly.
And the closer the splits crowd, the less room there is for that kind of
error.

\begin{note}
The second split can be found exactly, with Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic}'s
slope used twice. Once round the two-cycle, a nudge is multiplied by the
slope at each of the cycle's two points, and a page of school algebra shows
that the product is $4 + 2r - r^{2}$. That reaches $-1$ when
$r^{2} - 2r - 5 = 0$, at $r = 1 + \sqrt{6} = \val{ch08.fork.two.exact}$; the
table's \val{ch08.fork.two} is early by the same kind of margin. The later
splits have no formula this simple.
\end{note}

\section{The top of the hump}
\label{sec:ch08-superstable}
\index{superstable cycle}\index{Feigenbaum, Mitchell}

\noindent
In 1975 Mitchell Feigenbaum, a physicist at the Los Alamos laboratory in New
Mexico, set out to pin these splits down more sharply, working on a
programmable pocket calculator. Watching the orbit settle was too slow and
too blunt, so he measured something better placed: not the split itself,
where a cycle hangs on by its fingertips, but the point in each stretch where
the cycle is held most firmly.

The slope of the logistic map at $x$ is $r(1 - 2x)$, the stretch factor
Chapter~\ref{ch:feedback} taught you to measure. It is zero at
$x = \num{0.5}$, the top of the hump. A cycle that passes through $\num{0.5}$
multiplies any nudge by that zero once per trip round, so it wipes the nudge
out completely. Such a cycle is called \emph{superstable}. For each period
$1, 2, 4, 8, \ldots$ there is exactly one value of $r$ on the road where it
happens, and this chapter calls those values \emph{landmarks}. Finding one is
a single equation in $r$: start at $\num{0.5}$, take the number of steps the
period says, and arrive back at $\num{0.5}$.

\begin{think}
For period one the question is: for which $r$ does one step take $\num{0.5}$
straight back to $\num{0.5}$? The step is $r \times \num{0.5} \times
\num{0.5}$.
\end{think}
\reveal{$r = 2$. Half of a half is a quarter, and $2 \times \num{0.25} =
\num{0.5}$.}
For longer periods there is no mental shortcut, and bisection does not need
one. The function \code{from\_top} reports how far from $\num{0.5}$ the orbit
ends, and that distance is positive on one side of a landmark and negative
on the other, so halving the bracket closes in on it:

\pyregion{code/ch08/superstable.py}{search}{Start at the top, and halve the bracket until the orbit comes back to it}{lst:ch08-search}

\noindent
The first two landmarks are bracketed by eye from the picture. After that,
each gap is smaller than the one before, so the next landmark is looked for
between a tenth and a half of the last gap further on \dash{} a generous
bracket, since the splits shrank by a factor near five. A \emph{family} here
is a function that, given $r$, builds the rule.

\pyregion{code/ch08/superstable.py}{landmarks}{The landmarks for periods $1, 2, 4, 8, \ldots$, each bracketed from the last}{lst:ch08-landmarks}

\noindent
The listing finds the first \val{ch08.landmark.count} landmarks, for periods
$1$ up to $1024$, and prints each gap and each gap divided by the next.
Nothing in it but $+$, $-$, $\times$ and $\div$, so every digit is the same
on every computer:

\transcript{ch08-superstable}

\noindent
Look down the last column. The ratio wobbles for a few rows and then stops
moving: \val{ch08.delta}. That is \emph{Feigenbaum's constant}, usually
written $\delta$ (the Greek letter delta), and the landmarks give it far more
sharply than the splits did, because at a landmark the orbit settles at once
instead of barely at all. The splits and the landmarks take turns along the
road, one of each per period, so both sequences crowd in at the same rate
and head for the same end, $r = \val{ch08.end}$. The period-two landmark,
\val{ch08.super.two}, is $1 + \sqrt{5}$ exactly, which is where the note's
product $4 + 2r - r^{2}$ is zero.

Feigenbaum published in 1978; Pierre Coullet and Charles Tresser in France
found the same number independently at about the same time. What he did
next is the reason this constant is famous.

\begin{lab}{l08_01_superstable}{Find a landmark yourself}

of steps of the logistic map, and \code{bisect}, which halves a bracket until
it is tiny. Put them together in \code{superstable} and check your answer for
period two against $1 + \sqrt{5}$. Then find the $r$ between \num{3.82} and
\num{3.84} at which $\num{0.5}$ comes back to itself after \emph{three} steps.
You will meet that number again in this chapter.
\end{lab}

\section{A number that belongs to the road}
\label{sec:ch08-universal}
\index{universality}\index{sine map}

\noindent
Feigenbaum's next step was to try a different rule. The \emph{sine map}
\[ x_{n+1} = s \sin(\pi x_{n}) \]
takes $x$ between $0$ and $1$ and returns $s$ times the sine of $\pi x$. You
do not need trigonometry to follow it: for $x$ between $0$ and $1$ the sine
of $\pi x$ is a smooth hump that rises from $0$ to $1$ at $x = \num{0.5}$ and
falls back to $0$. So the sine map has the logistic map's shape \dash{} one
hump, its top at $\num{0.5}$ \dash{} and nothing else of its formula: no
$x(1-x)$, a different knob $s$, and a curve that is not a parabola. Its
bifurcation diagram shows the same kind of road, with the splits at
different places.

\begin{think}
Run the same landmark search on the sine map. Do you expect its gaps to
shrink by the logistic map's \val{ch08.delta} again, or by some other number
that belongs to the sine?
\end{think}
\reveal{The same number. The listing below finds the sine map's gaps
shrinking by \val{ch08.sine.first} at first, then closing in, landmark by
landmark, on \val{ch08.sine.delta}, where the logistic map's ratios also
settle.}
The listing puts both searches side by side. The only thing it knows about
the sine map is its family and two brackets read off its diagram:

\pyregion{code/ch08/two_maps.py}{compare}{The same search on two unrelated rules}{lst:ch08-compare}

\transcript{ch08-two-maps}

\noindent
The two roads end in different places (the last row: \val{ch08.end} for the
logistic map, \val{ch08.sine.end} for the sine map) and start differently,
yet the ratio they settle on is the same to every digit the table prints.
The \code{ratios} function behind the table divides each gap by the next;
the second lab asks you to write it.

\plotfig{ch08-ratios}{Each gap between landmarks divided by the next, for two
rules with nothing in common but a single smooth hump. They start apart and
end on the same number.}{the ratios for two rules converging}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A different equation gives a different route.}} It gives a
different map of the road \dash{} the splits happen at different values of
the knob \dash{} but the road has the same shape, and the rate at which its
splits crowd together is the same number. That number belongs to the route,
not to the equation. This is \emph{universality}, and it is why a constant of
nature can come out of a toy model.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
Why should that be? Each new split is decided by the behaviour of the map
over a smaller and smaller stretch near the top of its hump, seen through a
stronger and stronger magnifying glass. Magnify any smooth, rounded hump
enough around its top and it looks like a parabola: the details that make
the sine map a sine have been magnified out of sight. What survives is the
one feature every such rule shares, and the ratio is a property of that
feature alone. The constants of school geometry, like $\pi$, belong to
shapes; this one belongs to a process.

\mermaidfig{ch08-universal}{Universality in one line: any rule with one
smooth hump takes the same road, and the road carries its own
ratio.}{any hump, one route, one ratio}

\noindent
It was a shock because it made a prediction about systems whose equations
nobody could write down. If a real system approaches chaos by period
doubling, its splits should crowd together at Feigenbaum's rate, whatever
it is made of.

\begin{worldbox}
Around 1980 Albert Libchaber and Jean Maurer in Paris heated a tiny box of
liquid helium from below and recorded the temperature at one point as the
fluid rolled over. As they turned up the heating, the rhythm of the
temperature doubled its period, and doubled again. Later experiments on
convecting fluids and on electronic circuits measured the ratio of
successive gaps and found numbers close to Feigenbaum's \dash{} not to four
decimals, because a real experiment can resolve only the first few
doublings, but close enough that nobody takes it for a coincidence. In 1986
Feigenbaum and Libchaber shared the Wolf Prize in physics.
\end{worldbox}

\begin{rigourbox}
Why every smooth hump gives the same $\delta$ is not proved here. Feigenbaum
explained it with a method from physics called \emph{renormalisation},
which compares the map after each doubling with a rescaled copy of itself;
a proof, with the help of a computer, was given by Oscar Lanford in 1982.
The word \emph{smooth} matters: a hump whose top is flatter than a parabola's
gives a different constant, so universality sorts rules into classes rather
than declaring them all alike.
\end{rigourbox}

\begin{lab}{l08_02_ratio}{Feigenbaum's ratio, and where the doublings end}

on the assumption that each is the one before divided by the last ratio.
The test feeds your functions the landmarks of both maps, so you can see
the same ratio arrive at two different ends.
\end{lab}

\section{Order inside the chaos}
\label{sec:ch08-windows}
\index{periodic window}\index{Li--Yorke theorem}

\noindent
Past $r = \val{ch08.end}$ the doublings are used up, and the diagram turns
to smear.

\begin{think}
From $r = \val{ch08.end}$ to $r = 4$, is the logistic map chaotic at every
setting of the knob, the way it is at \num{3.9}?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. Look at the picture again: the smear is streaked with pale
stripes, and in each stripe the orbit is back to a short cycle. The widest,
near \num{3.83}, is a window of period three.}
\code{code/ch08/windows.py} walks $r$ across that stripe with the same
\code{settled\_period} as before, then runs the landmark search on the
cycles inside it:

\transcript{ch08-windows}

\noindent
At \num{3.8284} the orbit never repeats; a ten-thousandth further on it has
settled into a three-cycle. The window opens at exactly $r = 1 + \sqrt{8}$,
which is \val{ch08.window.open}, and the three-cycle then doubles to six and
to twelve and dissolves into smear again before $\num{3.86}$. Inside the
window, in other words, the whole road repeats in miniature: its landmarks
crowd together at a ratio of \val{ch08.window.delta}, Feigenbaum's number
once more, now from periods $3, 6, 12, \ldots$ instead of $1, 2, 4,
\ldots$.

\plotfig{ch08-zoom}{Left: the period-three window. Right: the middle branch
of the window, magnified. It is a small copy of the whole bifurcation
diagram, with its own cascade, its own smear and its own
windows.}{the period-three window, magnified}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Once a system has reached chaos, it is chaotic everywhere
beyond that point.}} Between $r = \val{ch08.end}$ and $4$ the logistic map
switches back to order infinitely many times, in windows of every period,
the widest opening at $1 + \sqrt{8}$. Turn the knob a little and a chaotic
system can become perfectly periodic, and turn it a little further and it is
chaotic again. Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov}'s exponent dips below zero in
exactly these stripes.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{note}
In 1975 Tien-Yien Li and James Yorke published a paper whose title was its
result: \emph{Period three implies chaos}. Paraphrased: if a continuous rule
takes an interval into itself, like the logistic map on $0$ to $1$, and some
starting point comes back to itself after exactly three steps, then the rule
has cycles of every length, and it has starting points whose orbits never
settle into any cycle at all. The paper gave the word \emph{chaos} its
mathematical meaning. A stronger result, ordering every period, had been
proved in 1964 by Oleksandr Sharkovsky in Kyiv, and was little known in the
West until after it. Inside the window almost every start ends on the
three-cycle, but the theorem still holds there: the other cycles and the
orbits that never settle are present, unstable, and an orbit can wander
among them for a long time before it finds its way home.
\end{note}

\noindent
The diagram has one more surprise, and it belongs to
Chapter~\ref{ch:mandelbrot}: this whole picture lies along a line through
the most famous image in mathematics, and the splits and the window sit
exactly where a one-line conversion says they should.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{bifurcation diagram} plots, for each value of the knob,
  where the orbit lives once the transient is over. A \textbf{bifurcation}
  is a value of the knob at which the long-run behaviour changes kind; on
  the logistic map's road each one is a \textbf{period doubling}.
\item The splits crowd together, each gap a little under five times the
  next, so infinitely many of them fit before $r = \val{ch08.end}$. A split
  measured by watching comes out early, because near a split the orbit
  settles very slowly.
\item At a \textbf{superstable} landmark the cycle passes through the top of
  the hump, where the slope is zero. Each landmark is one equation, solved by
  \textbf{bisection} with nothing but $+$, $-$, $\times$ and $\div$, and the
  ratio of their gaps settles on \textbf{Feigenbaum's constant},
  \val{ch08.delta}.
\item The sine map gives the same ratio. The number belongs to the route
  shared by every rule with one smooth hump, not to any formula:
  \textbf{universality}. It has been measured in convecting fluids and in
  circuits.
\item Beyond the cascade, chaos is interrupted by \textbf{windows} of order.
  The widest, of period three, opens at $1 + \sqrt{8}$ and holds a miniature
  of the whole road; Li and Yorke proved that period three implies chaos.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{checkpoint}
\item In a bifurcation diagram, what does the column of dots above one value
  of $r$ show? What does it look like for a fixed point, for a two-cycle and
  for chaos?
  \answerto{Where the orbit lives in the long run at that $r$, once the
  transient is thrown away: one dot for a fixed point, two for a two-cycle,
  and a smear of different values for chaos.}
\item Two successive gaps between splits are \num{0.08} and \num{0.017}.
  Roughly what is their ratio, and how long would you expect the next gap
  to be?
  \answerto{About \num{4.7}, since $\num{0.08} \div \num{0.017}$ is close to
  it; so the next gap should be about $\num{0.017} \div \num{4.7}$, which is
  roughly \num{0.0036}.}
\item Why does a split located by watching the orbit settle come out slightly
  early?
  \answerto{Near a split the cycle that is losing its grip multiplies a nudge
  by nearly $-1$ each time round, so it settles extremely slowly. A run of
  finite length still shows it flipping between values and reads it as the
  doubled period.}
\item What is a superstable landmark, and why can it be found to many digits
  with nothing but $+$, $-$, $\times$ and $\div$?
  \answerto{The value of $r$ at which the cycle passes through the top of the
  hump, $x = \num{0.5}$, where the slope is zero. It is the solution of one
  equation \dash{} start at $\num{0.5}$, take the period's number of steps,
  and be back at $\num{0.5}$ \dash{} which bisection solves by halving a
  bracket.}
\item The sine map's doublings run out at about \val{ch08.sine.end}, the
  logistic map's at about \val{ch08.end}. Does that contradict universality?
  \answerto{No. Universality says the ratio of successive gaps tends to the
  same number, about \val{ch08.delta}, for every rule with one smooth hump;
  where each road ends depends on the rule.}
\item Is the logistic map chaotic at $r = \num{3.83}$? What did Li and Yorke
  prove about a rule with a cycle of period three?
  \answerto{No: \num{3.83} lies in the period-three window, which opens at
  $1 + \sqrt{8}$, about \val{ch08.window.open}, and the orbit settles into a
  three-cycle. Li and Yorke proved that a continuous rule taking an interval
  into itself with a point of period three has cycles of every length, and
  starting points whose orbits never settle into any cycle.}
\end{checkpoint}
