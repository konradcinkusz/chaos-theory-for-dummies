\chapter{The rule of the next step}
\label{ch:iteration}
\index{iteration}\index{rule!of the next step}

\noindent
Your doctor prescribes one tablet every morning. Each tablet puts 100
milligrams of the drug into your blood, and by the next morning your body
has cleared half of whatever was there. Take one a day for a month, a year,
ten years: does the amount in your blood keep climbing for as long as you
take them?

Hold on to your answer. The question is about a rule applied over and over
\dash{} halve, add a tablet; halve, add a tablet \dash{} and rules of the
same kind run savings accounts, cooling tea, populations, loans and a hotel
shower. Chapter~\ref{ch:models} called \code{temp = next\_minute(temp)} the
most important line in the book. This chapter is about what that line does
when you run it for ever, and it ends with you able to say what a whole
family of rules will do in the long run without running them at all.

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{write a rule of the next step in the book's notation and work out
  its orbit, by hand and in Python}
\outcome{recognise the straight-line rule behind savings, medicine and
  cooling, and read its long-run behaviour from one number}
\outcome{answer a \enquote{how many steps until} question with a logarithm}
\outcome{find where a rule stands still, and decide whether it stays there
  when nudged}
\outcome{measure the slope of a rule at a point, and read a cobweb diagram}
\end{outcomes}

\section{Today, tomorrow, the day after}
\label{sec:ch02-rule}
\index{state}\index{orbit}

\noindent
Put 1000 pounds into an account that pays 5 per cent a year, and leave it
alone. After one year the bank adds 50, which is 5 per cent of 1000, and
you have 1050. After the second it adds 5 per cent of 1050 and you have
\val{ch02.save.two}; after the third, \val{ch02.save.three}. Every year the
same thing happens to whatever is there.

Call the balance after $n$ years $x_n$, read \enquote{$x$ sub $n$}: the
small $n$ says which year and is not multiplied by anything. So $x_0 = 1000$
is where you start and $x_1 = 1050$ is a year later, and the bank's rule is
\[ x_{n+1} = \num{1.05}\, x_n , \]
next year's balance is \num{1.05} times this year's, every year.

Every stepping rule in this book has this shape. There is a \emph{state}, a
number $x$ saying where things are now; a \emph{rule} $f$, which stands for
\enquote{whatever you do to the state to get the next one}; and the next
state, which is $f$ applied to this one:
\[ x_{n+1} = f(x_n) . \]
When the step number does not matter the rule is written with an arrow,
$x \mapsto \num{1.05}\,x$, read \enquote{$x$ goes to \num{1.05} times $x$}.
The list $x_0, x_1, x_2, \ldots$ is the \emph{orbit} of $x_0$, and
producing it, by feeding each answer back in as the next question, is
\emph{iteration}.

\mermaidfig{ch02-feed-back}{Iteration. The rule turns the state now into
the next state, which becomes the state now; the same rule is used at every
step.}{the loop of iteration}

\begin{think}
Take the rule $x \mapsto 2x + 1$: double, then add one. Starting from
$x_0 = 1$, what are $x_1$, $x_2$ and $x_3$?
\end{think}
\reveal{$3$, $7$ and $15$. Twice $1$ plus one is $3$; twice $3$ plus one
is $7$; twice $7$ plus one is $15$.}
There is no way to $x_3$ except through $x_1$ and $x_2$: a hundred steps
ahead takes a hundred steps, tedious by hand and effortless for a
machine.

\code{orbit} is the loop of Chapter~\ref{ch:models} with the bookkeeping
done once:

\noindent
Its partner \code{linear(a, b)} hands back the rule $x \mapsto a\,x + b$,
ready to give to \code{orbit}.
\end{projectbox}

\section{One straight line, three fates}
\label{sec:ch02-fates}
\index{linear rule}\index{rule!linear}

\noindent
The savings rule only multiplies. Many rules multiply and then add:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The tea} of Chapter~\ref{ch:models} loses a tenth of its gap
  to a room at 20 degrees each minute: from $x$ it goes to
  $x - \num{0.1}(x - 20)$, which tidies up to $\num{0.9}\,x + 2$.
\item \textbf{The tablets}: halve, then add 100, or
  $x \mapsto \num{0.5}\,x + 100$.
\item \textbf{A population} in which births outnumber deaths by a fifth
  each year: $x \mapsto \num{1.2}\,x$.
\item \textbf{A rumour} that everyone who knows it passes to one new person
  a day: $x \mapsto 2x$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
All of them have one shape, $x \mapsto a\,x + b$: multiply by a fixed
number $a$, then add a fixed number $b$. Its graph, with the state now along
the bottom and the next state up the side, is a straight line, so it is
called a \emph{linear} rule \dash{} although, as you are about to see, what
it does over time is mostly curved. Start four of them from the same place:

\pyregion{code/ch02/fates.py}{rules}{Four linear rules, iterated side by side from the same start}{lst:ch02-fates}

\transcript{ch02-fates}

\noindent
The first column doubles for ever and the second halves towards nothing.
The third creeps up on $10$ from below. The fourth settles on $10$ too, but
by jumping from side to side, each jump smaller than the last: it is the
third column mirrored. Those are the three fates of a linear rule \dash{}
grow without bound, shrink to nothing, or settle \dash{} and a negative
multiplier adds alternation, but not a fourth fate.

\plotfig{ch02-three-fates}{The three fates of a linear rule, from the same
start. One grows without bound, one dies away, and two settle on the same
number, one creeping up on it and one jumping from side to
side.}{three linear rules and their three fates}

\begin{think}
Back to the savings account. You want the balance after ten years without
taking ten steps. What single calculation gives it?
\end{think}
\reveal{Multiply 1000 by \num{1.05} ten times over: $x_{10} =
\num{1.05}^{10} \times 1000$, which is \val{ch02.save.ten}.}
The small raised $10$ is a \emph{power}: $\num{1.05}^{10}$ is \num{1.05}
multiplied by itself ten times. When a rule only multiplies, $x \mapsto
a\,x$, its orbit has a formula, $x_n = a^n x_0$, and you can jump to any
step. Growth in which every step multiplies by the same number is called
\emph{exponential}; its shrinking twin, with $a$ between $0$ and $1$, is
\emph{exponential decay}.

\section{Slow explosions, and how many steps until}
\label{sec:ch02-until}
\index{exponential growth}\index{doubling time}\index{logarithm}
\index{half-life}

\noindent
\enquote{Exponential} has become a word for \enquote{fast}. Here is a test
of that.

\begin{think}
Two savings accounts both start with 1000. Account A adds 50 every year.
Account B grows by 2 per cent a year, which is exponential growth. Which is
ahead after thirty years?
\end{think}
\reveal{Account A, with $1000 + 30 \times 50 = 2500$ against B's
\val{ch02.acc.grows.thirty}. B does not overtake until year
\val{ch02.acc.cross}.}
If you chose B, you reasoned from the word rather than the rule: 2 per
cent of 1000 is 20, less than A's 50, and B needs decades of growing on its
own growth to catch up.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Exponential growth means fast growth.}} It means growth in
proportion to what is already there, and the multiplier can be very close
to one. At 1 per cent a year a sum takes about \val{ch02.dbl.one.log}
years to double. What exponential growth guarantees is not speed but
inevitability: however small the rate, it overtakes any growth that adds a
fixed amount \dash{} eventually, and eventually can be a lifetime.
\end{trapbox}

\plotfig{ch02-two-accounts}{Adding a fixed amount against growing by a fixed
fraction. The exponential account is behind for most of a century, and then
it is not.}{linear against exponential growth}

\noindent
The useful question about exponential growth is \enquote{how many steps
until it doubles?}, and the answer does not depend on the start.

\begin{think}
At 7 per cent a year, roughly how many years does a sum take to double:
about 5, about 10, or about 14?
\end{think}
\reveal{About 10. Counting whole years, 1000 first passes 2000 in year
\val{ch02.dbl.seven.count}; the precise answer is \val{ch02.dbl.seven.log}
years.}
There is a button for this. The question is: for which $n$ is
$\num{1.07}^n = 2$? A \emph{logarithm} answers exactly that kind of
question. Your calculator has one marked \code{ln}, the natural logarithm,
and the one thing you need to know about it is what it does to a power:
\[ \ln (a^n) = n \ln a . \]
It turns \enquote{multiply by $a$, $n$ times} into \enquote{add $\ln a$, $n$
times}. Take the logarithm of both sides of $a^n = 2$ and you have
$n \ln a = \ln 2$, so
\[ n = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln a} : \]
the number of steps is the logarithm of how far you want to go, divided by
the logarithm of one step. With $\ln 2 = \val{ch02.ln.two}$ and
$\ln \num{1.07} = \val{ch02.ln.seven}$, that is \val{ch02.dbl.seven.log}.

\pyregion{code/ch02/doubling.py}{count}{How long until a balance doubles, counted and by the logarithm}{lst:ch02-doubling}

\transcript{ch02-doubling}

\noindent
The count is the logarithm rounded up. At 2 per cent that is
\val{ch02.dbl.two.log} against \val{ch02.dbl.two.count}: after 35 years
the balance is \val{ch02.dbl.two.short} times what went in, a hair short of
double. The last column, the \emph{rule of 70}, divides 70 by the
percentage; it works because the logarithm of one step is close to a
small rate itself ($\ln \num{1.07}$ is nearly \num{0.07}) and
$100 \times \ln 2$ is about 70.

Shrinking works the same way. A drug of which the body clears a fifth every
hour keeps \num{0.8} each hour, and the time until half is left, its
\emph{half-life}, is $\ln \num{0.5} / \ln \num{0.8}$, about
\val{ch02.half.fifth} hours.

logarithm the same question, for growth and for decay. Then write
\code{half\_life}. Python's natural logarithm is \code{math.log}.
\end{lab}

\section{Where the rule stands still}
\label{sec:ch02-fixed}
\index{fixed point}

\noindent
Now the opening question. The state is the drug in your blood right after
the morning tablet, in milligrams: $x \mapsto \num{0.5}\,x + 100$, starting
from $x_0 = 100$.

\begin{think}
Work out the first few mornings: $100$, then half of that plus $100$, and
so on. Does the amount keep climbing for as long as you take the tablets?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. It goes $100$, $150$, $175$, \num{187.5}, \num{193.75}, and on,
closing in on $200$ without ever reaching it.}
Why $200$? Because the rule leaves $200$ alone: half of $200$ is $100$, and
the tablet puts the $100$ straight back. A state the rule does not change is
a \emph{fixed point}, and you find it by asking which $x$ satisfies
\[ x = f(x) . \]
For the tablets, $x = \num{0.5}\,x + 100$; take $\num{0.5}\,x$ from both
sides and $\num{0.5}\,x = 100$, so $x = 200$. For any linear rule the same
steps give
\[ x^{*} = \frac{b}{1 - a} , \]
the star marking the fixed point. (If $a$ is exactly $1$ the rule only
keeps adding, and stands still nowhere.) The tea's rule has its fixed point
at $2 / \num{0.1} = 20$ degrees: the temperature of the room.

\pyregion{code/ch02/drug.py}{tablets}{One tablet a morning, and the gap to the fixed point}{lst:ch02-drug}

\transcript{ch02-drug}

\noindent
The shortfall in the right-hand column halves every morning, because the
body halves whatever is there and the tablet adds the same $100$ regardless.
After \val{ch02.drug.within} days you are within one milligram of $200$,
and you will never be exactly there. The amount does not climb for ever; it
levels off at a fixed point.

\begin{worldbox}
Pharmacologists call this fixed point the \emph{steady state}, and the rule
of thumb taught to doctors and pharmacists is that a medicine taken at
regular intervals reaches it after four or five half-lives. That is the
table above: after four half-lives the shortfall has halved four times,
leaving the level at \val{ch02.drug.four} per cent of the steady state, and
after five at \val{ch02.drug.five}. When a medicine is needed at full
strength at once, a doctor may give a larger first dose, a \emph{loading
dose}, which starts the orbit on the fixed point instead of below it.
\end{worldbox}

\section{Staying put, and running away}
\label{sec:ch02-stability}
\index{fixed point!stable}\index{fixed point!unstable}\index{stability}

\noindent
Here is a fixed point of a different character. You owe \num{10000} on a
loan charging 1 per cent a month, and you repay 100 a month, so the balance
goes to $\num{1.01}\,x - 100$ each month.

\begin{think}
What happens to the balance over the years? And what if you owe
\num{11000} instead, with the same interest and the same repayment?
\end{think}
\reveal{At \num{10000}, nothing, ever: the interest is exactly 100 and your
100 cancels it. At \num{11000} the interest is 110, your 100 does not cover
it, and after ten years you owe \val{ch02.loan.up}.}
The rule's fixed point is \num{10000}, but it does not pull you there.
Start a thousand above and the debt runs away upwards; start a thousand
below, at \num{9000}, and it runs away downwards, to zero, paid off after
\val{ch02.loan.payoff} months, about \val{ch02.loan.years} years.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A fixed point is where the system ends up.}} It is where
the system stays if it is exactly there. Whether a system near it moves
towards it or away is a separate question, and the loan's fixed point
pushes every other balance away. A fixed point that draws nearby states in
is \emph{stable}; one that pushes them out is \emph{unstable}. Finding a
fixed point tells you where to look; only its stability tells you whether
anything will be there.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
The difference is one number. Follow the gap between a state $x$ and the
fixed point $x^{*}$ through a step:
\[ f(x) - x^{*} = (a\,x + b) - (a\,x^{*} + b) = a\,(x - x^{*}) . \]
The first equals sign uses the fact that $x^{*}$ is fixed, so $a\,x^{*} +
b$ is $x^{*}$ itself. The $b$ cancels, and what is left says: \textbf{each
step multiplies the gap by $a$}. The tablets halve it; the loan grows it by
1 per cent a month; the tea, with $a = \num{0.9}$, is why the two cups of
Chapter~\ref{ch:models} drew closer by a tenth every minute.

What matters is the size of $a$, written $\abs{a}$, which ignores the
sign: $\abs{\num{-0.5}} = \num{0.5}$. Below 1 the gap shrinks and the fixed
point is stable; above 1 it grows and the fixed point is unstable. The sign
says only whether the gap keeps its side or flips.

You have felt a negative $a$ in a hotel shower. You want 38 degrees, the
water is at 30, and you overdo it: each turn of the tap moves the
temperature by one and a half times the error, the other way. The rule is
$x \mapsto x - \num{1.5}(x - 38)$, which is $x \mapsto \num{-0.5}\,x + 57$.
From 30 you go to 42, 36, 39, \num{37.5}: too hot, too cold, too hot, each
miss half the last. Jerk the tap by two and a half times the error and $a =
\num{-1.5}$: 30, 50, 20, 65, worse every time.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lXX@{}}
\toprule
the multiplier $a$ & a nudge away from $x^{*}$ & the orbit \\
\midrule
above $1$ & grows & runs away, on one side \\
between $0$ and $1$ & shrinks & creeps in, from one side \\
between $-1$ and $0$ & shrinks, flipping side & settles, overshooting \\
below $-1$ & grows, flipping side & runs away, swinging wider \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\section{The slope: how much a nudge is multiplied}
\label{sec:ch02-slope}
\index{slope}

\noindent
The multiplier $a$ has a second name, which the rest of the book uses. Draw
the tablets' rule as a graph, the state now along the bottom and the next
state up the side: the straight line $y = \num{0.5}\,x + 100$. Move along
the bottom from 150 to 151, one unit, and the line rises from 175 to
\num{175.5}, half a unit. The \emph{slope} of a graph at a point is exactly
this: \textbf{how far the output moves for each unit the input moves}. A
straight line has the same slope everywhere, and for $x \mapsto a\,x + b$
that slope is $a$. So the last section's finding, in the words the rest of
the book uses, is that \emph{a small error is multiplied by the slope at
every step}.

A curve has a slope too, but it differs from point to point, as a hill is
steep in one place and nearly flat in another. You measure it at a point by
nudging: move the input a tiny amount $h$ either side of $x$, see how far
the output moves, and divide,
\[ \text{slope at } x \approx \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h} , \]
where $\approx$ means \enquote{very nearly equal}. In \pkg{chaoslab}'s
\code{slope} the nudge $h$ is one millionth: rise over run, for a run too
short for the curve to bend.

\pyregion{code/ch02/slope.py}{measure}{The slope measured by nudging, on a line and on a curve}{lst:ch02-slope}

\transcript{ch02-slope}

\noindent
The line's slope is \num{0.5} wherever you measure it; the curved rule
$x \mapsto x \times x$ gets steeper the further right you go.

\begin{think}
The square rule has slope $3$ at \num{1.5}. You meant to feed it \num{1.5}
but fed it \num{1.501}, an error of \num{0.001}. Roughly how wrong is the
output?
\end{think}
\reveal{By about \num{0.003}: the error is multiplied by the slope.
Exactly, $\num{1.501} \times \num{1.501} - \num{2.25} =
\val{ch02.nudge.exact}$.}
That is the fact behind every gap in this chapter, now on a curve: an error
is multiplied by the slope where it happens to be. On a line the factor
never changes, which is why this chapter can foresee everything. On the
square rule it does: between $0$ and \num{0.5} the slope is below 1 and
errors shrink, beyond \num{0.5} it is above 1 and they grow. So here is a
question this chapter cannot answer. What does an orbit do if its rule is
curved, so that it keeps passing from places that shrink errors to places
that stretch them? Chapter~\ref{ch:feedback} bends the line, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic} follows an orbit that does exactly this.

\section{Seeing it: the cobweb diagram}
\label{sec:ch02-cobweb}
\index{cobweb diagram}

\noindent
You can watch iteration happen on the rule's own graph, with a pencil and
no arithmetic. Draw the rule's line and the diagonal $y = x$, whose height
always equals its position along the bottom. Then:

\begin{enumerate}
\item From $x_0$ on the bottom axis, go up or down to the line. The height
  you reach is $x_1$.
\item Go across to the diagonal, where height equals position: you now
  stand above $x_1$, ready to use it as the next input.
\item Up or down to the line gives $x_2$; across, and so on.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The corners of the path are the orbit, drawn. \pkg{chaoslab}'s
\code{cobweb} lists them; for the tablets, starting from an empty
bloodstream:

\pyregion{code/ch02/cobweb_steps.py}{corners}{The corners of the cobweb for the tablets}{lst:ch02-cobweb}

\transcript{ch02-cobweb}

\noindent
The corners on the diagonal carry the orbit, each number twice: $0$,
$100$, $150$, $175$. The path can rest only where the line crosses the
diagonal, because there $x = f(x)$: the crossing is the fixed point, seen.

\begin{think}
The loan's line, $y = \num{1.01}\,x - 100$, is steeper than the diagonal.
Starting a little to one side of the crossing, does the path climb into the
crossing or away from it?
\end{think}
\reveal{Away. Each step multiplies the gap to the crossing by \num{1.01}, so
the path lands a little further out every time, on whichever side it
started.}

\plotfig{ch02-cobweb}{Cobwebs on three lines that cross the diagonal at
$200$. A slope below one in size draws the path into the crossing \dash{}
a staircase if the slope is positive, a web if it is negative \dash{} and a
slope above one draws it out.}{a cobweb on a line}

\noindent
When the slope is negative the path winds round the crossing, and that web
gives the diagram its name. The three panels are the table of
Section~\ref{sec:ch02-stability}, drawn.

So you have something rare in this book: complete foresight. Given
$x \mapsto a\,x + b$ and a start, you can say without taking a step what it
will do for ever \dash{} settle at $b/(1 - a)$ if $\abs{a} < 1$, run away if
$\abs{a} > 1$, alternate if $a$ is negative \dash{} and how long it will
take. That certainty is what the rest of the book takes away: bend the line
and a rule one line long can do things no formula here foresees.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{rule of the next step} turns the state now into the next,
  $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$. Repeating it is \textbf{iteration}; the list of
  states is the \textbf{orbit}.
\item Savings, cooling and dosing follow a \textbf{linear rule},
  $x \mapsto a\,x + b$: it grows without bound, shrinks to nothing, or
  settles; a negative $a$ makes it \textbf{alternate}.
\item \textbf{Exponential} growth is proportional, not necessarily fast.
  The \textbf{logarithm} says how many steps a change by a factor $r$
  takes, $\ln r / \ln a$: the doubling time and the half-life.
\item A \textbf{fixed point} is a state the rule leaves unchanged: solve
  $x = f(x)$, which for a linear rule gives $b/(1 - a)$.
\item Each step multiplies the gap to the fixed point by $a$: it is
  \textbf{stable} if $\abs{a} < 1$ and \textbf{unstable} if $\abs{a} > 1$,
  and nothing ends up at an unstable one.
\item The \textbf{slope}, how far the output moves per unit the input
  moves, is the factor a small error is multiplied by in one step. A
  curve's slope differs from point to point.
\item The \textbf{cobweb diagram} draws an orbit on the rule's graph; on a
  line it closes in on the crossing with the diagonal when the slope is
  below one in size.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}
