\chapter{Why you need chaos: a field guide to prediction}
\label{ch:why}
\index{prediction!field guide}\index{forecast}

\noindent
Here are four predictions you might meet in a single week. A table says
there will be a total eclipse of the Sun on 2 August 2027. A weather app
gives a 70 per cent chance of rain on Saturday. A pension statement says
what your savings will be worth in 2060. And a computer program answers a
question about next year's interest rates without a flicker of doubt.

Which of them would you trust, and how far? You can now answer that with
numbers you computed yourself. This chapter turns the book into a field
guide: five questions to ask of any prediction, each answered by a
measurement, and then the case for why everybody needs them.

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{ask of any prediction whether a small change in its start grows}
\outcome{compute a prediction horizon, and say what better data buys}
\outcome{say what can be predicted beyond the horizon, and read odds}
\outcome{separate chaos from noise, and a small cause from a big effect}
\outcome{apply the field guide's seven questions to a forecast or a model}
\outcome{explain, with numbers you computed, why some things can be
  predicted for centuries and others only for days}
\end{outcomes}

\section{Question one: does a small change grow?}
\label{sec:ch18-grow}
\index{sensitive dependence!test for}

\noindent
Start with the cleanest pair in the list: the eclipse and the rain. Both are
forecast the same way. A computer takes a measured present, applies exact
laws of physics, and steps forward in time.

\begin{think}
An eclipse table is good for thousands of years and a detailed weather
forecast for about a week. Both come from exact laws stepped forward from a
measured start. In the language of this book, what is the difference?
\end{think}
\reveal{How fast a small error in the start grows. In the motions of the
Earth and the Moon it grows so slowly that it stays small for millennia; in
the air it grows until it is as large as the weather itself.}
The test is the one Chapter~\ref{ch:butterfly} turned into a measurement:
run the rule twice, from two starts closer than any instrument could tell
apart, and watch the gap. Here are three rules the book has met, each
started twice a billionth apart:

\pyregion{code/ch18/grows.py}{twice}{The first question, asked of three rules}{lst:ch18-twice}

\transcript{ch18-grows}

\noindent
The cooling tea forgets its disturbance: the gap is multiplied by
\num{0.9} every minute. The logistic map at $r = \num{2.8}$ forgets too,
because it settles to its fixed point. At $r = 4$ the billionth grows until
it is as big as the map allows. If the gap shrinks, the path is predictable
for as long as the rule is right. If it grows, the next question is how
fast.

Question one has a question in front of it: is there an exact rule at all?
For the planets and for Lorenz's three equations there is. For an economy or
an epidemic the rule itself changes as people react to the forecast, which
is a different difficulty from chaos.

\section{Question two: how far is the horizon?}
\label{sec:ch18-horizon}
\index{prediction horizon}\index{Lyapunov exponent}

\noindent
A growing error usually grows by roughly the same factor each step, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov} turned that factor into one number: the Lyapunov
exponent, written $\lambda$ (the Greek letter lambda), which is the natural
logarithm $\ln$ of the average factor. A factor of $2$ is an exponent of
$\ln 2$, about \val{ch18.ln.two}. Suppose your start is wrong by $e_0$ and
the forecast is useless once the error passes a tolerance $T$. After $n$
steps the error is $e_0$ times the factor $n$ times over, so it reaches $T$
when
\[ n \approx \frac{\ln(T / e_0)}{\lambda}. \]
In words: the horizon is the logarithm of how many times too small your
error is, divided by the exponent. The book's library has it in one line
(\code{e**exponent} there is the factor, rebuilt from the exponent):

\pyregion{code/src/chaoslab/measure.py}{horizon}{The horizon formula in the book's library}{lst:ch18-horizon-lib}

\noindent
Try it on the logistic map at $r = 4$, whose exponent this chapter measures
as \val{ch18.lam.logistic} per step ($\ln 2$, as
Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov} shows). Know the start to six decimal places, so
$e_0$ is a millionth, and call the forecast useless once it is out by more
than $T = \num{0.1}$. Then $T / e_0 = 100\,000$, and $n \approx
\val{ch18.ln.six} / \val{ch18.lam.logistic} \approx \val{ch18.hor.six}$
steps.

\begin{think}
You buy an instrument a thousand times more precise, and now know the start
to nine decimal places. How long does the forecast last: a thousand times as
long, about three times as long, or a few steps longer?
\end{think}
\reveal{A few steps longer: about \val{ch18.hor.gain} more, so
\val{ch18.hor.nine} in all. Dividing $e_0$ by a thousand adds $\ln 1000 /
\lambda = \val{ch18.ln.thousand} / \val{ch18.lam.logistic}$ steps, and
nothing more.}
That is the formula's answer. Here is the map's own, measured the long way
over \val{ch18.apart.starts} different starts:

\pyregion{code/ch18/thousandfold.py}{measure}{A thousand times better data, measured}{lst:ch18-thousandfold}

\transcript{ch18-thousandfold}

\noindent
\plotfig{ch18-digits}{How far ahead a forecast lasts, against how many
decimal places of the start are right. Lines are the formula, dots the
median of many measured pairs of runs. Each extra digit buys the same few
steps.}{horizon against correct digits}

\noindent
The formula said \val{ch18.hor.six} and \val{ch18.hor.nine}; the map says
\val{ch18.apart.six} and \val{ch18.apart.nine}. Every extra correct digit
buys the same number of steps, $\ln 10 / \lambda = \val{ch18.ln.ten} /
\val{ch18.lam.logistic}$, about \val{ch18.per.digit} here. The horizon
grows like the logarithm of your precision, so better instruments and
bigger computers can only add a fixed stretch. A small calculator puts three
of the book's systems side by side:

\pyregion{code/ch18/horizon.py}{calculator}{The horizon calculator}{lst:ch18-calculator}

\transcript{ch18-horizon}

\noindent
The Hénon map's errors grow more slowly, so each digit buys it
\val{ch18.per.digit.henon} steps; Lorenz's system, at
\val{ch18.lam.lorenz} per unit of time, gains \val{ch18.per.digit.lorenz}
units of time per digit. Plotted against the number of correct digits,
every horizon is a straight line (Figure~\ref{fig:ch18-digits}).

The same arithmetic explains the eclipse. Jacques Laskar's 1989 computation
of the solar system, quoted in Chapter~\ref{ch:mechanics}, gave the inner
planets an exponent of about one fifth per million years: a Lyapunov time,
one over the exponent, of about five million years. Know the Earth's
position to a metre, and call the forecast useless when the error is the
size of its orbit, 150 million kilometres. The horizon is five million
years times $\ln 150\,000\,000\,000 \approx \val{ch18.planets.ln}$, some
\val{ch18.planets.hor} million years, and a millimetre would add only about
\val{ch18.planets.gain} million. The solar system is chaotic; on the scale
of an eclipse table its errors have not yet begun to matter.

backwards. Then ask how many digits a hundred steps of the logistic map

\end{lab}

\section{Question three: what survives the horizon?}
\label{sec:ch18-beyond}
\index{ensemble forecast}\index{attractor!statistics}

\noindent
Past the horizon the path is lost. That is not the same as knowing nothing,
and it is the part of chaos theory most often missed.

\begin{think}
Forty steps ahead, the logistic map's path is lost several times over. Is
there anything about step forty you can still predict?
\end{think}
\reveal{Yes: the odds. The chance that $x$ is below \num{0.25} at step
forty is about one in three, and so is the fraction of time any long run
spends there. The path is gone; its statistics are not.}
Forecasters get such odds by running the model many times, from starts
spread across everything the measurement allows, and counting. That is an
ensemble, and Chapter~\ref{ch:weather} builds one for the weather. Here is
the smallest version:

\pyregion{code/ch18/beyond.py}{ensemble}{A forecast made from an ensemble, and one long run}{lst:ch18-ensemble}

\transcript{ch18-beyond}

\noindent
With the start right to six digits, all \val{ch18.ens.members} runs agree
until step \val{ch18.ens.sure.six}, and the forecast is one certain answer.
Then they spread, the forecast becomes odds, and within a few steps the
odds settle at \val{ch18.ens.clim}: the fraction of time one long run spends
below \num{0.25}, from either start. With nine digits the runs agree until
step \val{ch18.ens.sure.nine}, and then the same thing happens
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch18-odds}).

\plotfig{ch18-odds}{An ensemble forecast of the chance that $x$ is below
\num{0.25}. It is certain while the runs agree, gives odds as they spread,
and settles at the long-run fraction. Three more digits delay this and do
not prevent it.}{a forecast turning into odds}

So a forecast of a chaotic system comes in three forms, one after another:
a path while the runs agree, odds as they spread, and the long-run
statistics after that. The last is what Chapter~\ref{ch:lorenz} calls the
statistics of the attractor, and for the weather it is the climate.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Unpredictable means unknowable.}} Beyond the horizon the
path cannot be known, and nearly everything else can: where the system can
go and where it cannot, how often it visits each region, the odds for any
question you care to ask. A 70 per cent chance of rain is not a forecaster
hedging; it is the correct answer to a question chaos has made impossible to
answer with a yes or a no. A climate projection forecasts statistics, not
Tuesdays.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{worldbox}
The \enquote{cone of uncertainty} the US National Hurricane Center draws
around a storm's forecast track widens with every day of lead time. It is
sized from the errors of the centre's own recent forecasts, so that the
storm's centre can be expected to stay inside it about two times in three:
a horizon and a set of odds in one picture, published for millions of
people to act on.
\end{worldbox}

\section{Question four: chaos, noise, or a small cause?}
\label{sec:ch18-noise}
\index{noise}\index{butterfly effect}

\noindent
Irregular data invites a story, and two stories need a question each.

The first says \enquote{this is chaos}. Chapter~\ref{ch:life} shows that a
chaotic series and a random one can share a histogram, and that structure
tells them apart: plot each value against the next and chaos draws a curve
where noise draws a cloud, and chaos can be forecast a step or two ahead
where noise cannot. It matters which you have, and how much data there is:
a short, noisy record can look like almost anything.

The second story runs backwards, from a big effect to a small cause.

\begin{think}
Nudge the cooling tea and the logistic map at $r = 4$ by a billionth each,
as in Section~\ref{sec:ch18-grow}. Forty steps later, which nudge has
changed something? Has it changed the fraction of time the map spends below
\num{0.25}?
\end{think}
\reveal{Only the logistic map's, and only its path: the gap has grown to the
size of the map. The fraction of time below \num{0.25} is still about a
third from either start. The tea's nudge has faded away.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Small causes always matter.}} In a stable system they die
away, as the tea's did. In a chaotic one a small cause changes the details
beyond the horizon and nothing about the statistics, and because every
other small cause changes the details too, no single one can be picked out
afterwards as the reason. The butterfly of Chapter~\ref{ch:butterfly} did
not cause the storm. Blaming one small cause for a big effect after the
fact is a story, not a measurement.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Question five: can it be steered?}
\label{sec:ch18-steer}
\index{control of chaos}\index{synchronisation}

\noindent
The last question turns the limit into a tool.

\begin{think}
If a tiny change in the start changes the future completely, is that bad
news or good news for somebody who wants to control the system?
\end{think}
\reveal{Both. It is why you cannot forecast the system far ahead, and it is
why a tiny push, well timed, can move it a long way.}
Edward Ott, Celso Grebogi and James Yorke made that the idea of their 1990
paper on controlling chaos: wait until the system passes near one of the
unstable cycles hidden in its attractor, nudge a parameter slightly, and it
can be held on that cycle. Chapter~\ref{ch:taming} does it with nudges
under one per cent, and makes two chaotic systems keep in step, as Louis
Pecora and Thomas Carroll showed in the same year. So chaos is no excuse
for fatalism. A system you cannot forecast may be one you can steer, and
changing a parameter, such as the forcing in a climate model, moves the
statistics even when no path can be forecast.

\section{Why everyone needs it}
\label{sec:ch18-everyone}
\index{chaos!why it matters}

\noindent
\emph{It ended the clockwork}, without adding a single random ingredient:
an exact rule and a nearly exact start give a future known only as far as a
horizon that grows like the logarithm of your precision.

\emph{It says which forecasts to trust, and for how long.} An eclipse and a
thunderstorm obey laws of the same kind; what differs is the exponent. That
teaches humility about long-range certainty, because a single number far
beyond the horizon is a guess dressed as a measurement, and trust in
short-range models, because inside the horizon they are astonishingly
good.

\emph{It makes probabilities respectable.} Odds are what a correct forecast
looks like beyond the horizon, not a failure of forecasting.

\emph{It is beautiful and universal.} Feigenbaum's constant comes out of a
line of algebra and out of real fluids and circuits
(Chapter~\ref{ch:bifurcations}); fractals give a coastline a dimension
between one and two (Chapter~\ref{ch:fractals}); the Mandelbrot set holds
the whole road to chaos (Chapter~\ref{ch:mandelbrot}).

\emph{And it is a resource}: control, synchronisation, and the scrambling a
computer's random numbers are designed to have (though
Chapter~\ref{ch:computers} warns that a chaotic map alone is a poor source
of them).

Chaos theory is not a theory of everything, nor of disorder in general:
most of the world's irregularity is noise, ignorance or a rule that keeps
changing.

\begin{think}
A colleague says: \enquote{The economy is chaotic, so any forecast is as
good as any other.} What is wrong with that?
\end{think}
\reveal{Both halves. Nobody has shown that an economy follows a fixed
chaotic rule, and even a chaotic system is not free to do anything: it
keeps to its attractor, its odds are fixed, and a short-range forecast of it
can be very good.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Chaos means anything goes.}} Chaos is an exact rule whose
motion stays inside a bounded attractor, with steady statistics and a
horizon you can compute. Calling something chaotic is a claim that can be
tested, not a licence to call anything unpredictable butterfly-shaped, and
not a reason to stop forecasting.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Laplace's demon, one last time}
\label{sec:ch18-demon}
\index{Laplace's demon}

\noindent
Chapter~\ref{ch:models} ended by planting a question, and you can now answer
it.

\begin{think}
Suppose the rule is exact and you know it perfectly. Is the future then
known, as far ahead as you like?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. It is known only as far as the horizon, which grows like the
logarithm of how precisely you know the start. Beyond it, what is known is
the attractor, the statistics and the odds.}
Laplace's demon needs an infinitely precise present, and every extra digit
buys it only a few more steps. The clockwork is real for the eclipse and the
tide, and it runs out for the weather, the double pendulum and a single line
of algebra.

That leaves the forecasts you meet every day: the train seven minutes away,
the rain on Saturday, the projection, the confident answer. Ask each the
five questions. You will trust some forecasts more than you used to and some
a great deal less, and for the first time you will be able to say why, with
a number.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Question one}: run the rule twice from starts a hair apart. If
  the gap shrinks, the path can be forecast as far as the rule is right.
\item \textbf{Question two}: the \textbf{horizon} is about $\ln(T/e_0)$
  divided by the exponent $\lambda$, so each correct digit buys the same few
  steps.
\item \textbf{Question three}: beyond the horizon the \textbf{statistics}
  and the \textbf{odds} survive, and an ensemble shows a path turning into
  odds.
\item \textbf{Question four}: irregular is not chaos until structure shows
  it, and a big effect cannot be pinned on one small cause afterwards.
\item \textbf{Question five}: sensitivity makes chaos \textbf{steerable}.
\item The \textbf{field guide} adds two questions and sorts familiar
  forecasts by horizon.
\item Chaos ended the \textbf{clockwork} and makes odds respectable; it is
  not a licence for \enquote{anything goes}.
\item \textbf{Laplace's demon} would know the future only to the horizon.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{checkpoint}
\item Two runs of a rule start a billionth apart; thirty steps later they
  are a trillionth apart. What does question one say?
  \answerto{The error shrinks, so the path can be forecast as far ahead as
  the rule itself is right.}
\item Errors in a system grow with an exponent of \num{0.5} per day. You
  know the start to within \num{0.001} and can tolerate an error of $1$.
  Given that $\ln 1000 \approx \val{ch18.ln.thousand}$, what is the horizon?
  \answerto{About $\val{ch18.ln.thousand} / \num{0.5} \approx \num{13.8}$
  days.}
\item You now measure that start ten times more precisely. Given that
  $\ln 10 \approx \val{ch18.ln.ten}$, how much longer does the forecast
  last?
  \answerto{About $\val{ch18.ln.ten} / \num{0.5} \approx \num{4.6}$ days
  more: a fixed extra stretch, not ten times as long.}
\item Far beyond its horizon, an ensemble of the logistic map at $r = 4$
  gives a one-in-three chance that $x$ is below \num{0.25}. Is that forecast
  worthless?
  \answerto{No. It is the correct forecast: the fraction of time the map
  spends there, the same from any start.}
\item A series of daily measurements looks irregular. Name two things to
  check before calling it chaos.
  \answerto{Whether each value plotted against the next draws a curve rather
  than a cloud; whether it can be forecast a step or two ahead from similar
  past moments; and whether the record is long enough to tell.}
\item Why does the property that ruins long-range forecasts of a chaotic
  system make it possible to steer?
  \answerto{It is sensitive to small changes: a tiny error grows, and so
  does the effect of a tiny, well-timed push.}
\item Explain why eclipses can be predicted for thousands of years and the
  weather only for about a week.
  \answerto{The horizon is $\ln(T/e_0)$ divided by the exponent. The solar
  system's exponent is about one fifth per million years, which gives a
  horizon of about a hundred million years; the atmosphere's errors grow as
  much in days. Better data adds a fixed stretch to either.}
\end{checkpoint}
