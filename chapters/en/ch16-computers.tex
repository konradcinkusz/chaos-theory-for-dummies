\chapter{Chaos inside the computer}
\label{ch:computers}
\index{floating point}\index{rounding}

\noindent
Every picture of chaos in this book was drawn by a computer, and so was
every number. Here is an awkward fact about the machine that drew them. It
cannot store the number $\num{0.1}$. It stores a number very close to it, and
every time it adds or multiplies two numbers it rounds the answer to one it
can store.

You have spent several chapters learning that chaos turns the smallest error
into a large one. So if the computer makes a tiny error at every step, how
can any of those pictures be right? This chapter measures the answer, which
is better than you might fear and worse than you might hope.

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{say what a computer stores when you type a number, and why
  $\num{0.1} + \num{0.2}$ does not come out as $\num{0.3}$}
\outcome{predict the step at which the doubling map dies on a computer}
\outcome{estimate how many steps a chaotic computation stays right from the
  number of binary digits it keeps}
\outcome{say which results of a chaotic simulation you can trust, and why}
\outcome{describe a random-number generator as a deterministic rule, and say
  why it must repeat}
\end{outcomes}

\section{What a computer keeps}
\label{sec:ch16-keeps}
\index{binary numbers}\index{double precision}

\noindent
Write one third as a decimal and you never finish: $\num{0.333}\ldots$, the
threes running on for ever. Decimals can write exactly only the fractions
whose bottom is built from twos and fives, the factors of ten. A computer
counts in twos instead. In \emph{binary} the places after the point are worth
a half, a quarter, an eighth and so on, so $\tfrac{3}{4}$ is written
$\num{0.11}$: a half plus a quarter.

\begin{think}
Which of these can binary write exactly, with a fixed number of places:
$\tfrac{1}{2}$, $\tfrac{3}{8}$, $\tfrac{1}{10}$?
\end{think}
\reveal{$\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $\tfrac{3}{8}$, but not $\tfrac{1}{10}$. Binary
writes exactly the fractions whose bottom is a power of two, and ten has a
factor of five.}
In binary one tenth is $\num{0.000110011}\ldots$, with the block $0011$
repeating for ever, so a computer has to cut it off. Python, like almost
every program you use, keeps a number as a \emph{double}:
\val{ch16.bits.double} significant binary digits, about
\val{ch16.decimal.double} decimal ones, plus a power of two that says where
the point goes. For one tenth it keeps the nearest double, which is too large
by about \val{ch16.tenth.err}. Every sum and product is then \emph{rounded}
too: the exact answer is replaced by the nearest double.

\begin{think}
Both $\num{0.1}$ and $\num{0.2}$ are stored slightly wrong, and their sum is
rounded once more. Does the computer agree that $\num{0.1} + \num{0.2}$ is
$\num{0.3}$?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. The sum prints as a number a whisker above $\num{0.3}$, and the
comparison answers \code{False}.}

\pyregion{code/ch16/floats.py}{store}{What the computer stores, and what it does with it}{lst:ch16-floats}

\transcript{ch16-floats}

\noindent
The third line is the exact value kept for $\num{0.1}$; the fourth writes it
as a fraction, whose bottom is $2^{\val{ch16.tenth.power}}$. Every double is
a whole number divided by a power of two. The sixth line adds three numbers
in two orders and gets two answers: once every step rounds, the grouping that
school said cannot matter does matter. The last shows the room running out:
past $2^{53}$ a double cannot hold every whole number.

\begin{note}
These rounding rules were fixed in 1985 in a standard called IEEE~754, which
almost every computer follows. It requires $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$ and the
square root to give the exact answer rounded to the nearest double. That is
why your computer should agree with every number this book prints: the book's
own measurements use only those operations.
\end{note}

\section{A map the computer cannot run}
\label{sec:ch16-doubling}
\index{doubling map}

\noindent
The simplest chaotic rule there is fits in a line: double the number, and if
the answer is $1$ or more, take $1$ away. In symbols, $x \to 2x \bmod 1$,
read \enquote{$2x$ with its whole part thrown away}. This \emph{doubling map}
doubles the gap between any two nearby starts at every step. In binary you
can watch it work: doubling moves every digit one place left, and taking away
$1$ drops the digit that crossed the point, so $\num{0.1011}$ becomes
$\num{0.011}$, then $\num{0.11}$, then $\num{0.1}$. The map reads out its
start one binary digit at a time.

\begin{think}
Work the doubling map by hand from $\tfrac{1}{10}$, in fractions, for five
steps. What does its orbit do for ever after?
\end{think}
\reveal{$\tfrac{1}{5}$, $\tfrac{2}{5}$, $\tfrac{4}{5}$, $\tfrac{3}{5}$,
$\tfrac{1}{5}$: it goes round the same four values for ever, and never
reaches $0$.}
The computer's $\num{0.1}$, though, is a whole number divided by
$2^{\val{ch16.tenth.power}}$: it has a last binary digit, and every step
pushes one digit out past the point.

\begin{think}
What will the computer's orbit of $\num{0.1}$ be after sixty steps?
\end{think}
\reveal{Exactly $0$. When the last binary digit leaves, at step
\val{ch16.collapse.tenth}, nothing is left, and $0$ doubled is $0$ for ever.}

\pyregion{code/ch16/doubling.py}{race}{The doubling map from one tenth, on the computer and in exact fractions}{lst:ch16-doubling}

\transcript{ch16-doubling}

\noindent
For forty steps the two agree to six decimal places; then the computer
drifts, and dies. A computed orbit can be wrong in its whole character: a
loop has become a fall to nothing.

The strange part is that the computer's arithmetic made no error at all.
Doubling a double and taking away $1$ never needs rounding, and the chapter's
measurement script checks, step by step, that the computer's orbit is exactly
the true orbit of the number it stored. What is wrong is the start. A storable
number has finitely many binary digits, so every storable start is swallowed
by $0$, while almost every true start has infinitely many digits and never
is. From each of the \val{ch16.collapse.starts} starts $\tfrac{1}{997}$,
$\tfrac{2}{997}$, and so on, the computer's orbit died in between
\val{ch16.collapse.min} and \val{ch16.collapse.max} steps, most often in
\val{ch16.collapse.mode}.

\plotfig{ch16-collapse}{The doubling map from one tenth. The truth (open
circles) loops for ever; the computer (dots) agrees for about forty steps,
drifts, and at step \val{ch16.collapse.tenth} falls to $0$ for
good.}{the doubling map dying on a computer}

\begin{lab}{l16_01_collapse}{When does the doubling map die?}

\code{x.as\_integer\_ratio()}, which gives the stored number as a whole
number over a power of two. The test checks that the two always agree.
\end{lab}

\section{One rule, two programs}
\label{sec:ch16-formulas}
\index{logistic map!on a computer}\index{reproducibility}

\noindent
The doubling map never rounds. The logistic map of
Chapter~\ref{ch:logistic} is more typical: nearly every step rounds. Here it
is at $r = 4$, written two ways.

\pyregion{code/ch16/two_formulas.py}{rules}{The logistic map at r = 4, written two ways}{lst:ch16-rules}

\noindent
Multiply out the bracket in the first and you get the second:
$4x(1 - x) = 4x - 4x^{2}$. On paper they are one rule.

\begin{think}
Start both from $\num{0.1}$ on the same computer and run them for seventy
steps. Will the two programs print the same numbers?
\end{think}
\reveal{No. They differ in the last binary digit from step
\val{ch16.formulas.first}, and by step \val{ch16.formulas.apart} they are
more than $\num{0.1}$ apart.}
The first formula rounds $1 - x$ and then a product; the second rounds
$4x^{2}$ and then a difference. Each error is about one part in $10^{16}$,
and the map doubles it every step. The right-hand column below is the truth:
the orbit of exactly one tenth, carried to a hundred significant digits.

\transcript{ch16-two-formulas}

\noindent
Which formula is right? Both, for forty steps. By step \val{ch16.wrong.a}
the first has left the truth, and by step \val{ch16.wrong.b} so has the
second. Neither formula is wrong; each is right for about fifty steps, and
after that it is following a different orbit.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A deterministic program always gives the same answer.}}
Run the same program on the same machine and it does, to the last digit. But
change the computer, the compiler, a maths library or the order in which a
long sum is added, and you change which roundings happen, as the two formulas
did. Compilers may rearrange arithmetic for speed, or fuse a multiplication
and an addition into one step with one rounding. In most programs the
difference stays in the last digit. In a chaotic one it reaches the first.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
Fifty is not an accident, and you can predict it.

\begin{think}
At $r = 4$ an error roughly doubles every step, as
Chapter~\ref{ch:butterfly} measured. A double's rounding error is about
$10^{-16}$, and ten doublings multiply an error by about a thousand. Roughly
how many steps until an error of $10^{-16}$ has grown to $\num{0.1}$?
\end{think}
\reveal{About fifty. From $10^{-16}$ to $10^{-1}$ is fifteen factors of ten,
and every three factors of ten take about ten doublings.
Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov}'s horizon, with the rounding error as the error in
the start, gives \val{ch16.predict.double}.}
In one sentence: at $r = 4$ the map uses up one binary digit of accuracy per
step, so a double's \val{ch16.bits.double} digits last about fifty steps.
\emph{Single precision}, which graphics cards and much machine-learning
software use because it takes half the memory, keeps \val{ch16.bits.single}
binary digits, about \val{ch16.decimal.single} decimal ones; the same arithmetic predicts \val{ch16.predict.single} steps,
and it lasts \val{ch16.wrong.single}.

\plotfig{ch16-parting}{Distance from the true orbit of one tenth, on a
logarithmic scale, for the logistic map at $r = 4$. Every run's error doubles
each step, along the dotted line, until it fills the whole interval: after
about twenty steps in single precision, fifty in double.}{two precisions
parting company}

\begin{lab}{l16_02_single}{Single against double}
Round a number to single precision by packing it into four bytes and reading
it back (the starter shows how). Write the logistic map at $r = 4$ with every
operation rounded that way, run it beside the double version, and find the
first step at which they differ by more than $\num{0.1}$.
\end{lab}

\begin{worldbox}
Machine learning meets this daily. A neural network is trained by adding up
millions of small numbers on a graphics card, whose parallel threads can
finish in a different order each time, so a sum rounds differently from run
to run. Two runs of the same program from the same random seed can end with
networks whose weights differ throughout \dash{} and which usually score
about the same. The documentation of PyTorch, a widely used library, says
that fully reproducible results are not guaranteed across platforms and
releases, and offers a switch that forces a fixed order, at a cost in speed.
\end{worldbox}

\section{Why more digits cannot save you}
\label{sec:ch16-exact}
\index{exact arithmetic}

\noindent
The obvious cure is to stop rounding. Python's \pkg{fractions} module keeps
every number as an exact fraction. One tenth stays one tenth, and
$4x(1 - x)$ from it gives $\tfrac{9}{25}$, then $\tfrac{576}{625}$, with no
error at all.

\begin{think}
From $\tfrac{9}{25}$ to $\tfrac{576}{625}$ the bottom went from $25$ to
$625 = 25 \times 25$. If it is squared at every step, what happens to its
number of digits?
\end{think}
\reveal{It roughly doubles at every step: squaring a number about doubles
its number of digits.}

\pyregion{code/ch16/more_digits.py}{exact}{Exact fractions: the bottom is squared at every step}{lst:ch16-exact}

\transcript{ch16-more-digits}

\noindent
After ten steps the bottom has \val{ch16.exact.ten} digits. After fifty it
would have about \val{ch16.exact.fifty}, and storing it would take some
\val{ch16.exact.fifty.tb} terabytes. Exact arithmetic gets every step right
and cannot finish. The last four lines try the practical cure: keep a fixed
but larger number of digits, with Python's \pkg{decimal} module, and compare
with a thousand-digit run. Sixteen digits last \val{ch16.right.sixteen}
steps, like a double; twice the digits last twice as long. Every decimal
digit buys about \val{ch16.right.per.digit} steps, because it is worth about
\val{ch16.right.per.digit} binary ones.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{More decimal places make a simulation exact.}} They make it
right for longer, by a fixed number of steps per digit, never for ever. To
follow a chaotic orbit for a thousand steps you must carry about three
hundred decimal digits, and know the start that precisely too, which no
measurement will give you. It is Chapter~\ref{ch:lyapunov}'s cruel arithmetic
seen from the computer's side: precision buys a horizon that grows by a
constant, not in proportion.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Wrong path, right picture}
\label{sec:ch16-shadow}
\index{shadowing}

\noindent
So every long chaotic computation in this book followed the wrong orbit. Did
it draw the wrong picture? The way to find out rests on one observation.

\begin{think}
Running the logistic map forwards doubles a small error every step, on
average. What does running it backwards, from each value to the one before
it, do to a small error?
\end{think}
\reveal{Halves it, on average. Whatever a forward step stretches, the
backward step squeezes.}
So start at the end of a computed orbit and walk back. Two numbers lead to
any value in one step, one below $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and one above; take the one on
the same side as the computed value. Walking back shrinks errors, so the walk
stays close to the computed orbit all the way to the start, and what it finds,
worked to four hundred digits, is a genuine orbit of the rule.

\pyregion{code/ch16/shadow.py}{back}{Walking a computed orbit backwards to find the true orbit it follows}{lst:ch16-back}

\transcript{ch16-shadow}

\noindent
The computed orbit of $\num{0.1}$ left the truth after \val{ch16.wrong.a}
steps. But a start differing from one tenth by about \val{ch16.shadow.start}
has an exact orbit that stays within \val{ch16.shadow.gap} of every one of the
\val{ch16.shadow.steps} computed values. The computer did not follow the
orbit it was asked for. It followed, faithfully, the orbit of a start so close
that no measurement could tell the two apart. That true orbit is its
\emph{shadow}.

\begin{rigourbox}
The \emph{shadowing lemma}, proved by Dmitri Anosov and Rufus Bowen, covers
systems that stretch in some directions and squeeze in others at every point,
at rates that never weaken (\emph{uniformly hyperbolic} ones, such as the
doubling map). It says that however closely you want a computed orbit to be
shadowed, a small enough error per step guarantees it, for ever. The logistic
map at $r = 4$ is not quite of that kind: near $x = \tfrac{1}{2}$ its curve is
flat, and there the backward walk stretches. For it, shadowing was confirmed
by careful computation over long but finite stretches, notably by Hammel,
Yorke and Grebogi in 1987. This book proves neither; the proofs live in
graduate texts on dynamical systems.
\end{rigourbox}

\noindent
Now the payoff. A shadow is a true orbit, so it spends its time as true
orbits do. The last two lines of the output measure that: over a million
steps the first formula spends the fraction \val{ch16.stats.a} of its time
below $\num{0.1}$, the second \val{ch16.stats.b}, and the exact answer, from
a formula this book does not derive, is \val{ch16.stats.exact}. Two programs whose paths parted after fifty steps
agree about where the orbit lives. That is why the lobe fractions of
Chapter~\ref{ch:lorenz} and the climate statistics of
Chapter~\ref{ch:weather} can be trusted when no single computed path can.

\section{Machines that must repeat}
\label{sec:ch16-random}
\index{random numbers}\index{linear congruential generator}

\noindent
One more difference between a computer and mathematics is the plainest.
Between $0$ and $1$ there are \val{ch16.count.single} single-precision
numbers and \val{ch16.count.double} doubles: enormous, and finite. So a
computed orbit must sooner or later meet a value it has met before, and since
the rule gives the same next value every time, it then goes round the same
loop for ever.

In single precision the loops are short enough to find. From $\num{0.1}$ the
logistic map at $r = 4$ wanders for \val{ch16.loop.tail} steps, then goes
round a loop of \val{ch16.loop.length} values for ever; \val{ch16.loop.starts}
different starts end in only \val{ch16.loop.kinds} different loops. The loop
spoils the statistics too: the single-precision run spends the fraction
\val{ch16.stats.single} of its time below $\num{0.1}$, not
\val{ch16.stats.exact}, as Figure~\ref{fig:ch16-statistics} shows. Doubles
loop as well, but their loops are far too long to reach, which is the
practical reason to compute chaos in double precision.

\begin{think}
The logistic map at $r = 4$ never repeats, covers the whole of $0$ to $1$ and
looks random at a glance. Would it make a good random-number generator?
\end{think}
\reveal{No, for three reasons this chapter has measured. Its values pile up
near $0$ and $1$: about a fifth of its time is below $\num{0.1}$, not a
tenth. Each value is fixed by the one before it. And on a computer it falls
into a loop.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A chaotic map makes a good random-number generator.}} Chaos
makes the future unpredictable to someone who does not know the start
exactly, which is part of what randomness needs and not all of it. A good
generator must also spread its values evenly, show no pattern from one value
to the next, and repeat only after a loop nobody could ever go round. Those
properties are designed with number theory and checked with statistical
tests; they do not come free with chaos.
\end{trapbox}

\noindent
A random-number generator is a deterministic rule on a finite set of values,
built to have one enormous loop. The simplest kind, the \emph{linear
congruential generator}, is one line: multiply by $a$, add $c$, and keep the
remainder after dividing by $m$. With $a = 5$, $c = 3$ and $m = 16$, from
$0$ you get $3$, then $5 \times 3 + 3 = 18$, remainder $2$, then $13$, $4$,
$7$, $6$, $1$, $8$, $11$, $10$, $5$, $12$, $15$, $14$, $9$ and $0$ again: all
sixteen possible values before a repeat. The doubling map is a generator of
this kind with $a = 2$ and $c = 0$, and on $k$ binary digits it dies in $k$
steps \dash{} the collapse of Section~\ref{sec:ch16-doubling} in a new
costume. Python's own generator, the Mersenne Twister of Makoto Matsumoto and
Takuji Nishimura (1998), has a loop of $2^{19937} - 1$ values. Because it is
deterministic, the same starting value, the \emph{seed}, gives the same
\enquote{random} numbers every time, which is how a random experiment is made
repeatable.

\begin{lab}{l16_03_loops}{Every orbit ends in a loop}
Write a linear congruential generator and a function that finds, for any
rule and start, how many steps the orbit takes to enter its loop and how long
the loop is. Try it on the generator above, then find the loop of the
single-precision logistic map for yourself.
\end{lab}

\noindent
The practical lesson fits in a sentence: never trust a single long run of a
chaotic computation. Run it again with the formula rearranged, with more
precision and less, with a smaller step, and believe what survives. The path
past fifty steps will not survive. The statistics will.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{double} keeps \val{ch16.bits.double} significant binary
  digits. Most decimals, $\num{0.1}$ among them, are stored inexactly, and
  every sum and product is \textbf{rounded}.
\item The \textbf{doubling map} reads out its start one binary digit at a
  time, so on a computer every orbit dies at $0$; the true orbit of one tenth
  loops for ever.
\item Two programs for one rule round differently and part company. At
  $r = 4$ the logistic map uses \textbf{one binary digit per step}: about
  fifty steps in double precision, twenty in single.
\item \textbf{Exact arithmetic} doubles its digits every step and cannot
  finish; \textbf{more digits} buy a fixed number of steps each.
\item A computed orbit is \textbf{shadowed} by a true orbit from a nearby
  start, so its \textbf{statistics} are right when its path is wrong.
\item A finite machine makes every orbit end in a \textbf{loop}; a
  random-number generator is a rule built to make that loop enormous.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{checkpoint}
\item Why does $\num{0.1} + \num{0.2}$ not equal $\num{0.3}$ on a computer?
  \answerto{Neither $\num{0.1}$ nor $\num{0.2}$ has an exact binary form, so

  $0$, and what does the true orbit of $\tfrac{3}{10}$ do?
  \answerto{At step \val{ch16.collapse.three}, one step per binary digit. The
  true orbit goes $\tfrac{3}{5}$, $\tfrac{1}{5}$, $\tfrac{2}{5}$,
  $\tfrac{4}{5}$, $\tfrac{3}{5}$ and loops for ever.}
\item Single precision keeps \val{ch16.bits.single} binary digits. Roughly
  how many steps of the logistic map at $r = 4$ is it good for?
  \answerto{About twenty (measured: \val{ch16.wrong.single}): the map doubles
  the rounding error every step, using up one binary digit per step.}
\item Run exactly from $\tfrac{1}{10}$, the bottom of the fraction has
  \val{ch16.exact.ten} digits after ten steps. About how many after twenty?
  \answerto{About \val{ch16.exact.twenty}: the digits double every step, and

  the last value, or the fraction of time spent below $\num{0.1}$?
  \answerto{The fraction of time. The path has followed a different orbit

\end{checkpoint}
