| 32 | Ch. 3 `sec:ch03-overshoot` | A limit to growth means growth stops smoothly. | With a gentle growth rate it does. With a strong one, or with a delay between cause and effect, the population overshoots its level and oscillates, and turned up further the swings need not die away at all. |
| 41 | Ch. 4 `sec:ch04-euler` | A smaller step makes a simulation right. | It makes it right for longer. A stepping method's error is made fresh at every step and piles up: Euler with steps ten times smaller gains the same energy on a spring, ten times later. |

| 53 | Ch. 5 `sec:ch05-may` | Irregular output needs a random or complicated cause. | A simple deterministic rule with no randomness in it can produce output as irregular as chance. The output alone does not say which of the two made it; that was May's 1976 warning to ecologists. |
| 61 | Ch. 6 `sec:ch06-lorenz` | If I run a chaotic model again, I will get something different. | Not from the same start: a chaotic rule gives the same future from the same start, to the last digit. What changes the future is a changed start, however small. |
| 62 | Ch. 6 `sec:ch06-lorenz` | The butterfly causes the tornado. | Lorenz's question was about prediction, not power: a tiny disturbance changes which weather happens, and since every other disturbance does the same, no single one of them is the cause. |
| 63 | Ch. 6 `sec:ch06-determinism` | Chaos means random. | A chaotic system obeys its rule exactly at every step. What looks like randomness is the rule reading out digits of the start that nobody knows. |
| 71 | Ch. 7 `sec:ch07-stretch` | To find the typical stretch per step, average the stretches. | Growth compounds, so the typical factor is the one that, used at every step, gives the same product. Average the logarithms of the stretches, not the stretches: a step of $\times 4$ and a step of $\times \tfrac14$ leave a gap where it was. |
| 72 | Ch. 7 `sec:ch07-horizon` | Ten times better data gives ten times longer forecasts. | The horizon is about $\ln(T/\delta)/\lambda$, so a tenfold better start buys a fixed $\ln 10/\lambda$ extra steps. For the logistic map at $r = 4$ a thousandfold improvement buys about ten steps, whatever the error was before. |
| 73 | Ch. 7 `sec:ch07-horizon` | A bigger computer will eventually make any system predictable. | A faster computer does not measure the start any better, and the horizon depends on the error in the start only through its logarithm. Doubling the horizon needs twice as many correct digits; a thousand steps of the logistic map at $r = 4$ would need the start to about three hundred decimal places. |
| 81 | Ch. 8 `sec:ch08-picture` | The change from order to chaos is a sudden switch. | On the logistic map's road, order breaks down in an infinite cascade of period doublings, each arriving sooner than the last; chaos begins where the cascade runs out, at $r \approx \num{3.5699}$. |
| 82 | Ch. 8 `sec:ch08-universal` | A different equation gives a different route to chaos. | Any rule with a single smooth hump takes the same road: its doublings sit at different values of the knob, but the gaps between them shrink by the same ratio, Feigenbaum's $\delta \approx \num{4.6692}$. |
| 83 | Ch. 8 `sec:ch08-windows` | Once a system has reached chaos, it is chaotic everywhere beyond that point. | Beyond the cascade the logistic map returns to order infinitely many times, in windows of every period; the widest, of period three, opens at $r = 1 + \sqrt{8}$. |
| 91 | Ch. 9 `sec:ch09-flow` | Chaos needs many variables. | Lorenz's model has three numbers and two multiplications, and it is chaotic. Three is the fewest a flow needs, because a path in a plane cannot cross itself; it is also enough. |
| 92 | Ch. 9 `sec:ch09-attractor` | A chaotic system can go anywhere. | Unpredictable is not unbounded. Every Lorenz run, from almost any start, is drawn onto the same bounded butterfly-shaped set, the attractor, and never leaves it. |
| 93 | Ch. 9 `sec:ch09-statistics` | If the path is unpredictable, nothing about it is predictable. | The path is unpredictable beyond about twenty time units, but the statistics of the attractor \dash{} the fraction of time on each wing, the average height, even the Lyapunov exponent \dash{} come out the same from every start. |
| 101 | Ch. 10 `sec:ch10-knead` | If a system can never leave a small region, it cannot be very sensitive to its start. | The region caps how \emph{big} an error can get, not how fast it grows. A tiny error doubles and doubles until it is as large as the region, and from then on two runs are as unrelated as two points dropped in it at random. |
| 102 | Ch. 10 `sec:ch10-area` | If the area keeps shrinking, the motion must be dying out. | Hénon's map multiplies every area by \num{0.3} each step because it squeezes hard in one direction while it stretches in another. A patch becomes a longer and thinner ribbon whose points keep moving for ever. |
| 103 | Ch. 10 `sec:ch10-zoom` | Zoom in far enough on any curve and it turns into a straight line. | That is true of smooth curves. Each line of a strange attractor splits under magnification into a bundle of lines, and each of those splits again: layers within layers at every scale. |
| 111 | Ch. 11 `sec:ch11-koch` | A coastline has a length; it is only hard to measure. | A length is what measurements settle on as the ruler shrinks, and a coast's measurements do not settle: the Koch curve's grow by $\tfrac{4}{3}$ every time the ruler shrinks by three. A coastline's length is a fact about the coast and the ruler together. |
| 112 | Ch. 11 `sec:ch11-dimension` | A dimension must be a whole number. | Counted from copies or from boxes, a dimension is a measurement, and it need not be whole: the Koch curve's is $\ln 4 / \ln 3$, between one and two, and the Cantor set's $\ln 2 / \ln 3$, between nothing and one. Whole numbers belong to smooth shapes. |
| 113 | Ch. 11 `sec:ch11-world` | Fractals are decoration: pretty pictures, no use. | Fractal branching in lungs, blood vessels and rivers does work: it packs a large surface or a long edge into a bounded space. Real objects are fractal only over a range of sizes, and a claimed dimension should say which. |
| 121 | Ch. 12 `sec:ch12-plane` | Complex numbers are imaginary, so they describe nothing real. | The name is history, not a description. A complex number is a point of the plane with a rule for multiplying that stretches and turns, so anything that stretches and turns \dash{} a wave, a rotation, an alternating current \dash{} is described by complex numbers. |
| 122 | Ch. 12 `sec:ch12-mset` | Infinite detail means infinite information. | Every detail of the edge of the Mandelbrot set follows from one line of algebra, $z \to z^{2} + c$, and one question: does the orbit of $0$ escape? The detail is generated, not stored, and a short description can have an endless consequence. |
| 123 | Ch. 12 `sec:ch12-logistic` | The Mandelbrot set is a picture of chaos. | Most of what is black is order: in the heart the orbit of $0$ settles on one point, and in every bulb on a cycle whose length the bulb fixes. The never-settling orbits of the logistic map sit on the thin edge, which on the real axis is the needle between its windows. |
| 131 | Ch. 13 `sec:ch13-double` | A pendulum is a simple machine, so its motion must be simple. | True for one pendulum, whose state is two numbers. Hang a second from it and the state is four numbers, and a release from high up never settles into a repeating pattern. |
| 132 | Ch. 13 `sec:ch13-apart` | A system that keeps its energy, with no friction and nothing random in it, cannot be chaotic. | Fixing the energy of a four-number state still leaves three directions to move in, which is enough for chaos. Two double pendulums a millionth of a radian apart part within seconds, with the energy of each constant to about one part in $10^{8}$. |
| 133 | Ch. 13 `sec:ch13-solar` | The solar system is a perfect clock. | Over thousands of years it is an excellent one. Laskar found the inner planets chaotic, with a Lyapunov time of about five million years, so where Earth will be on its orbit a hundred million years from now cannot be predicted. |
| 134 | Ch. 13 `sec:ch13-solar` | A chaotic solar system is an unstable one: the planets will fly apart. | Unpredictable is not unstable. Computations over billions of years find the planets keeping orbits of much the same size and shape in almost every run; only their positions along those orbits cannot be foretold. |
| 141 | Ch. 14 `sec:ch14-ensemble` | A forecast is a single number. | A forecast is a number and how far to trust it. The same ensemble mean deserves confidence on a day the members agree and a shrug on a day they scatter, and the spread of the ensemble tracks the size of the error without anyone seeing the truth. |
| 142 | Ch. 14 `sec:ch14-rain` | A 30 per cent chance of rain, followed by rain, was a wrong forecast. | One day cannot refute a probability. A forecast of 30 per cent is right if it rains on about 30 per cent of the days it is issued; that check, reliability, needs many forecasts, and a good ensemble passes it. |
| 143 | Ch. 14 `sec:ch14-climate` | If the weather cannot be predicted, the climate cannot be predicted either. | Weather is the path and climate is the statistics of the attractor. Two runs that disagree about every day agree about the average, the swing and how often it rains; a change in the forcing moves those statistics, which is the question a climate projection asks. |
| 151 | Ch. 15 `sec:ch15-twins` | A random-looking sequence must come from randomness. | The logistic map at $r = 4$ looks as random as a shuffle of its own values and has no chance in it at all. Looking random is a fact about the look; only a test of order says anything about the cause. |
| 152 | Ch. 15 `sec:ch15-life` | Irregular data proves chaos. | Chaos and noise are both irregular. A claim of chaos needs evidence of a rule: structure in the return map, a forecast that starts good and worsens at a steady rate, and a record long and clean enough for either to mean something. |
| 153 | Ch. 15 `sec:ch15-evidence` | Chaos in markets means you can predict them. | Chaos is forecastable only a few steps ahead and not at all beyond its horizon, so chaotic prices would promise a short horizon with a hard limit, not a crystal ball; and the evidence for low-dimensional chaos in prices is weak and contested. |
| 154 | Ch. 15 `sec:ch15-evidence` | A good forecast one step ahead proves chaos. | A series with memory, like daily temperature, is forecastable a day ahead with no chaos at all. The signature of chaos is a forecast that is good a step ahead and worsens at a steady rate, and that beats surrogates keeping the series' slow ups and downs. |
| 161 | Ch. 16 `sec:ch16-formulas` | A deterministic program always gives the same answer. | On the same machine, with the same compiler and libraries, it does. Change any of those, or the order of a sum, and the roundings change; a chaotic computation carries a last-digit difference to the first digit in about fifty steps. |
| 162 | Ch. 16 `sec:ch16-exact` | More decimal places make a simulation exact. | Each extra digit buys a fixed number of correct steps, never all of them: at $r = 4$ the logistic map uses up one binary digit per step, and exact fractions double their digits at every step. |
| 163 | Ch. 16 `sec:ch16-random` | A chaotic map makes a good random-number generator. | Chaos gives unpredictability and nothing else a generator needs: the logistic map's values pile up near $0$ and $1$, each fixes the next, and on a computer it falls into a short loop. Good generators are designed with number theory to have one enormous loop. |
| 171 | Ch. 17 `sec:ch17-nudge` | Chaos cannot be controlled. | Chaos forbids prediction far ahead, and a controller only needs to predict one step: wait until the orbit passes near an unstable orbit, then cancel each coming miss with a nudge to a parameter. The logistic map at $r = \num{3.9}$ is held at its unstable fixed point with nudges under one per cent. |
| 172 | Ch. 17 `sec:ch17-small` | Controlling a system means overpowering it. | The controller supplies none of the motion: it keeps one of the system's own unstable behaviours by cancelling small misses, and once the orbit is caught the nudges shrink to almost nothing. Force, a large permanent change of the parameter, cannot even give the same state. |
| 173 | Ch. 17 `sec:ch17-sync` | Two chaotic systems can never agree. | Left to themselves they never stay together. Coupled so that one listens to the other, they can agree exactly: a copy of the Lorenz system handed the original's $x$ falls into step with it. The agreement is with the present, not a prediction of the future. |
| 181 | Ch. 18 `sec:ch18-beyond` | Unpredictable means unknowable. | Beyond the horizon the path is lost, and nearly everything else is known: where the system can go, how often it visits each region, and the odds. A forecast given as odds is the correct answer, not a hedge. |
| 182 | Ch. 18 `sec:ch18-noise` | Small causes always matter. | In a stable system they die away. In a chaotic one a small cause changes the details beyond the horizon and nothing about the statistics, and no single small cause can be blamed for a big effect after the fact. |
| 183 | Ch. 18 `sec:ch18-everyone` | Chaos means anything goes. | Chaos is an exact rule confined to a bounded attractor, with steady statistics and a horizon you can compute. Calling something chaotic is a claim to be tested, not a reason to stop forecasting. |
